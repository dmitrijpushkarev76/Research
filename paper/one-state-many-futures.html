One State, Many Maximal Futures

A scalar autonomous equation whose origin admits exactly one solution defined for all future time, and continuum-many that cannot be extended any further in forward time.

Abstract

For the initial-value problem ẋ = f(x), x(0) = 0 with f continuous, f(0) = 0 and f > 0 off the origin, the structure of the solution set is classical: it was characterised by Wallach [14] in 1948 and treated in general form by Binding [4] in 1979. This note takes one concrete member of that class, f(x) = |x|2/3 + x², and works it out completely.

The example has exactly one solution defined on all of [0,∞), namely x ≡ 0. It also has continuum-many solutions that admit no forward extension: for each waiting time T ≥ 0 there is a solution that rests at the origin until T, departs, and reaches +∞ at T + 3√2π/4. Every non-trivial branch terminates in finite time, which is exactly why the first count is one; the second is 𝔠 for an independent reason, that the equilibrium can be left at any moment. Each hypothesis moves one count, and only their conjunction separates the two.

The distinction this illustrates — between uniqueness among globally defined solutions and uniqueness among solutions that merely cannot be extended — is not introduced here either. It is discussed for general relativity by Earman [7], Smeenk and Wüthrich [13] and Doboszewski [5], for Newtonian blow-up by Wilson [15], and within a broader formulation of indeterminism by Azhar and Namjoo [2]. What is offered here is a minimal worked example in which every quantity is available in closed form.

1Introduction

Here is a question with a less obvious answer than it looks.

If an initial state admits exactly one solution that exists for all future times, does it follow that the state has a unique possible future history?

Not necessarily — not if histories are taken to be solutions that cannot be extended any further, rather than solutions defined at every future time. The two classes can differ, and where they do, the uniqueness verdict can differ with them. That is the whole content of this note, and none of it is new.

We use history throughout for a solution regarded as a candidate physical evolution. Nothing in §§2–5 depends on that identification; §7 is where it does work, and says so.

The mathematics is classical. For scalar autonomous equations the solution set through an equilibrium was characterised by Wallach [14] in 1948, and the general theory — the exact conditions for departure from an equilibrium, for escape to infinity in finite time, and for the structure of the solution set — is given by Binding [4]. The conceptual point is also familiar. In general relativity the maximal globally hyperbolic development of initial data is unique while extensions past a Cauchy horizon are not, and whether to restrict attention to the well-behaved class is a live question rather than a settled one [7, 13]. In Newtonian mechanics Wilson [15] argues that failures of global existence are descriptive gaps rather than failures of determinism, in explicit opposition to Earman and Norton. Azhar and Namjoo [2] argue that a uniqueness-based criterion misses the sense in which a terminating history is itself a failure of determination.

What follows is therefore expository. Its purpose is to put the distinction into a small explicit example and work it out in full: a single scalar equation, elementary integrals, a blow-up time in closed form, an explicit solution family. No general relativity, no N-body mechanics, no partial differential equations, no geometry.

2The example

Throughout we study

ẋ = f(x),   x(0) = 0,(IVP)

for a continuous f : ℝ → ℝ. A solution is a differentiable x : I → ℝ on an interval I with non-empty interior satisfying ẋ(t) = f(x(t)) throughout (one-sided derivatives at endpoints belonging to I). We work forward from 0 until Appendix A.

The example is

f(x) = |x|2/3 + x².

Four properties matter, and all four are elementary.

Properties of the example

(i) Continuity and the equilibrium. |x|2/3 is the composition of x ↦ |x| with s ↦ s2/3 on [0,∞), so f is continuous on ℝ; f(0) = 0; and f(x) > 0 for x ≠ 0, since |x|2/3 > 0 and x² ≥ 0.

(ii) Not locally Lipschitz at the origin. |f(x) − f(0)|/|x| = |x|−1/3 + |x| → +∞ as x → 0, so no Lipschitz constant works on any neighbourhood of the origin. Away from it, f′(x) = ⅔·sgn(x)|x|−1/3 + 2x is continuous, so f is C1 on ℝ∖{0} and the failure sits at exactly one point.

(iii) The substitution. For x > 0 put x = u³, a C1 increasing bijection of (0,∞). Then dx = 3u² du, x2/3 = u², x² = u6, and

dx / (x2/3 + x²) = 3u² du / (u²(1 + u⁴)) = 3 du/(1 + u⁴).

(iv) Both integrals converge. Near the origin, ∫01 3 du/(1+u⁴) ≤ 3. At infinity, ∫1∞ 3 du/(1+u⁴) ≤ ∫1∞ 3u−4 du = 1.

The two halves of (iv) do different work: convergence near the origin is what allows a solution to leave the equilibrium at all, and convergence at infinity is what makes the departing solution reach +∞ in finite time. Both facts are classical, and §6 says whose they are. Because the substitution rationalises the integrand completely, the total is available in closed form:

The blow-up time

T∗ = ∫0∞ dx/(x2/3 + x²) = 3∫0∞ du/(1 + u⁴) = 3 · (π/4)/sin(π/4) = 3√2 π / 4 ≈ 3.3321622036.

The last step is the standard evaluation ∫0∞ du/(1+un) = (π/n)/sin(π/n) at n = 4. That closed form is a convenience, and not a reason to prefer this f to other members of the class. Some of them do better. For f(x) = √|x|(1+|x|) one has F(x) = 2 arctan√x, so T∗ = π exactly, and every branch is elementary: xT(t) = tan²((t−T)/2) on (T, T+π). The f used here admits no such closed form for φ itself, which is why the figure below is drawn by inverting F numerically. Nothing in what follows depends on the choice; any member of the class would serve.

3The solution family

Define, for x > 0,

F(x) = ∫0x du/f(u),

which by (iii)–(iv) is a C1 strictly increasing bijection from (0,∞) onto (0, T∗) with F(0+) = 0. Let φ = F−1, extended by φ(0) = 0. Then φ is defined on [0, T∗), satisfies φ′ = f(φ) there, and φ(t) → +∞ as t → T∗−.

One detail deserves a line, because it is what lets the pieces glue. Since φ(t) → 0 as t → 0+ and f is continuous with f(0) = 0, we have φ′(t) = f(φ(t)) → 0; by the mean value theorem the right derivative of φ at 0 exists and equals 0 = f(φ(0)). So φ is C1 up to and including the origin, and leaves it with zero slope.

Now for each waiting time T ∈ [0, ∞) set

xT(t) = 0 for 0 ≤ t ≤ T,    xT(t) = φ(t − T) for T < t < T + T∗,

and let x∞ ≡ 0 on [0,∞). Each xT solves (IVP): on the two open pieces by construction, and at t = T because the left derivative is 0, the right derivative is φ′(0+) = 0, and f(xT(T)) = f(0) = 0. The join is C1, not merely continuous.

In words: the solution rests at the equilibrium as long as it likes, leaves with zero slope at a time of its choosing, and then — after exactly T∗ = 3√2π/4 more, the same for every branch — runs off to +∞.

Four members of the family, departing at T = 0, 1.6, 3.2, 4.8. Each curve is φ, obtained by inverting F numerically for the drawing only: the blow-up time is the analytic T∗ = 3√2π/4, and nothing here is offered as evidence for it. The curves are drawn only up to x = 6; that ceiling is graphical, and each branch in fact runs to +∞ as t approaches T + T∗. Along the axis is x∞, the one solution still defined at every later time.

4Globally defined versus forward-maximal

Two classes of forward solution, kept apart.

The two classes

ℋglob+(0) is the set of solutions of (IVP) defined at every t ≥ 0.

ℋmax+(0) is the set of solutions of (IVP) on intervals I ⊆ [0,∞) with min I = 0 admitting no extension to a solution on a strictly larger J ⊆ [0,∞). We call these forward-maximal; §5 explains why the prefix is not optional.

Plainly ℋglob+(0) ⊆ ℋmax+(0): a solution on [0,∞) has nowhere further to go inside [0,∞).

In this example the classical classification recalled in §6 takes a particularly transparent form.

The two counts

ℋglob+(0) = { x∞ },    ℋmax+(0) = { xT : T ∈ [0,∞) } ∪ { x∞ },

so |ℋglob+(0)| = 1 while |ℋmax+(0)| = 𝔠.

Why

Since f ≥ 0 everywhere, every solution is non-decreasing, so its zero set is a closed subinterval of its domain containing 0 — say the part of the domain in [0,T], with T the supremum of that set. If T = ∞ the solution is x∞. If T < ∞ then T lies in the domain (otherwise the solution is identically 0 on [0,T) and extends by 0, so is not forward-maximal), and beyond T the solution is strictly positive — it cannot be negative, being non-decreasing and zero at T. There ẋ/f(x) = 1; integrating from s to t and substituting u = x(σ) gives F(x(t)) − F(x(s)) = t − s, and letting s ↓ T, using continuity and the convergence of F at 0, gives F(x(t)) = t − T, i.e. x(t) = φ(t − T). So the domain lies in [0, T + T∗), and forward-maximality makes it equal. Distinct T give distinct solutions because T∗ > 0, so the two differ on an interval of positive length. Finally every xT with T < ∞ has bounded domain and so is not globally defined, leaving x∞ alone in the first class. Finally |[0,∞)| = 𝔠, and adjoining the single further element x∞ leaves the cardinality unchanged. ∎

Each hypothesis moves one count. Without convergence at the origin no branch could leave, and both counts would be 1; without convergence at infinity every xT would live forever, and both would be 𝔠. Only the conjunction separates them.

5Three senses of “maximal”

The word carries at least three meanings in the surrounding literature, and conflating them produces false statements about this example. We separate them once and then keep the prefix.

SenseMeaningIn this example
Forward-maximalNo extension within [0,∞).The xT, on [0, T + T∗). This is the class counted in §4.
Maximal (standard)No extension to any larger interval of ℝ.No xT is maximal in this sense. Setting x(t) = 0 for t < 0 always extends it, since f(0) = 0. Appendix A classifies the genuinely maximal solutions.
Maximal (extremal)The pointwise largest solution.x0, which departs immediately; the pointwise smallest is x∞. Hartman [10, ch. III §2] uses the word in this sense.

So [0, T + T∗) is a forward-maximal interval and not a maximal interval, and ℋmax+(0) is not a set of maximal solutions.

On the particular choice of f: we have not found a prior publication using |x|2/3 + x² to illustrate this point, though the search behind that remark is bounded and we make no priority claim. What can be said without qualification is that the choice gives a compact closed-form blow-up time. It is not the most economical member of the class — §2 names one that is — and we claim no advantage for it beyond convenience.

7Bearing on determinism

The example admits at least three readings. None of them is introduced here. We set them out with attributions and do not adjudicate between them.

First, what is being tested. We do not propose a definition of determinism; we borrow one already in use, so that the readings below have something definite to disagree about. Doboszewski [5, p. 2] adopts the criterion of Butterfield (1989) — his DM2 — under which a theory is deterministic for a given kind of region when any two models agreeing on a region of that kind agree throughout, so that, in his gloss, “to find a witness of indeterminism one needs merely to find two models that are isomorphic in region S but are not isomorphic overall — just as standard Laplacian intuitions demand.” Transposed to the present setting with all due looseness, the region is the initial state x(0) = 0, the models are the solutions through it, and the question is whether agreement there forces agreement throughout. Everything then turns on what “throughout” ranges over — which is precisely the choice §4 makes visible. Other criteria are in circulation and give different verdicts on this example; we take no stand on which is correct, and the example is not evidence for any of them.

Reading 1 · The global-solution criterion

If a physical history must be defined at every time, the branches xT are not histories, the state x = 0 has exactly one future, and the example says something about an initial-value problem and nothing about determinism. This is close to the position Wilson [15, p. 182] defends for Newtonian mechanics: failures of global existence are “descriptive holes”, and “it is as descriptive holes such issues should be addressed, not as failures of determinism.” He names Earman and Norton as holding the contrary view and calls their classification “terminologically unfortunate”.

Reading 2 · The maximal-history criterion

If a solution that cannot be extended counts as a history even though it ends, the same state has continuum-many futures, and requiring totality looks like a stipulation that secures determinism rather than a discovery that it holds. The structure of this objection is familiar from general relativity, where the maximal globally hyperbolic development of initial data is unique but, in Earman’s phrase [7, §6.4], often “not maximal simpliciter”, and the larger extensions are not unique. Smeenk and Wüthrich [13] organise their treatment around whether global hyperbolicity should be imposed by fiat, established from weaker assumptions, or justified from outside the theory — their §5 is titled “Global Hyperbolicity by Fiat” — and note that their analysis “runs parallel to recent discussions of determinism in Newtonian particle mechanics.”

A closer analogue, though only an analogue, is Doboszewski’s discussion [5, p. 12] of spacetimes that simply stop — Minkowski space with a spacelike slice and its future removed — for which he coins Doomsday indeterminism, remarking that “there is no physical reason for spacetime to come to an end abruptly in this way.” The comparison should be kept limited and it is not an equivalence: his truncations are produced by excising the future by hand, whereas here the finite endpoint is generated by the equation. That difference bears on his own desideratum, paraphrased from Earman and Norton, that “determinism may fail, but (one hopes) if it fails, it should fail because of reasons of physics, not because of a clever mathematical trick” [5, p. 2]. We claim no priority over that discussion and do not suggest the two constructions are of a kind.

Reading 3 · Termination as its own failure

A third position holds that the uniqueness-based formulation is the wrong test in the first place. Azhar and Namjoo [2] propose that indeterminism arises when future states are “(i) not unique …; (ii) not specified; or (iii) ‘incoherent’”, and argue that a Laplacian criterion based on non-uniqueness alone “does not comprehensively capture the relevant sense of a lack of determination”. On such a view the branches here witness a failure of determination by ending, independently of how many of them there are.

What happens at T + T∗

The mathematical fact is bare: xT(t) → +∞ as t ↑ T + T∗, and no continuation past that time exists. What that is remains open, and at least three answers are available:

(a) the branch is a history that terminates at a finite time;
(b) the divergence signals that the model has broken down and is no longer describing anything;
(c) +∞ is not a state, so the solution leaves the state space and simply ceases to exist.

The mathematics is indifferent: the counts of §4 hold under all three, because they are statements about a set of solutions and not about what solutions represent. The determinism verdict is not indifferent. The example bears on determinism only under (a), or under a criterion on which failure of continuation counts against determinism however the failure is described. We therefore make no unconditional claim that any physical theory is indeterministic. Under (b) or (c) the example remains exactly what §4 says it is, and nothing more.

What the example adds to this debate

As to the positions, nothing. What it adds is a test case in which the three readings give visibly different verdicts about one state of one system, with no idealisation to argue over: the equation is scalar and autonomous, f is continuous everywhere and C1 away from one point, and every quantity is exact.

One point is common to all three readings: the distinction is not dissolved by demanding more regularity. The Lipschitz failure sits at the equilibrium alone, and the branches terminate because the equation is superlinear at infinity, which is ordinary behaviour.

8What this note contributes

The phenomenon is classical and the distinction has substantial precedent, so the point of the example is not a theorem or a new criterion of determinism. It is that the whole thing is cheap: a scalar autonomous equation, elementary integrals, an exact blow-up time, an explicit family that can be drawn, and no general relativity, N-body mechanics or partial differential equations needed to check any step. Where the standard illustrations of the two failure modes are Norton’s dome [11] for non-uniqueness and the non-collision singularities of Xia [16] for finite-time escape, here both occur in one equation that fits on a line.

Limitations. Everything above concerns a scalar autonomous equation; the monotonicity argument uses the scalar order structure and does not survive to systems without new work. Nothing here is a theorem about Newtonian N-body dynamics. Appendix B gives a one-dimensional mechanical system exhibiting the mechanism, but it realises the mechanism, not this f. The interpretive question of §7 is left open, as it is in the literature cited there.

9Conclusion

The example shows, in the simplest setting we could find, why a statement about uniqueness must specify the class of solutions being quantified over. Uniqueness among globally defined solutions and uniqueness among solutions that cannot be extended are different properties of the same law, and here they disagree at the same state, by the largest margin available.

That lesson is already present in the broader literature, in both the mathematical and the foundational strands cited above. The contribution here is its explicit scalar illustration.

AAppendix · Two-sided time

The forward account says nothing about x < 0, and for this f, which is even, the negative half-line is populated: solutions can also arrive at the origin from below in finite time. Accounting for them identifies the solutions maximal in the standard sense and confirms that they are not the objects counted in §4.

Write S∗ = ∫−∞0 du/f(u). Two conditions are involved, and they are logically separate:

(a) ∫−10 du/f < ∞ — finite-time access to the equilibrium from below;
(b) ∫−∞−1 du/f < ∞ — finite-time escape to −∞ in backward time.

For an even f both follow from the forward conditions; in general neither follows from anything on the positive side. Take f(x) = x2/3 + x² for x ≥ 0 and f(x) = |x| + x² for x < 0: this is continuous, the forward conditions hold because the positive half-line is unchanged, and since 1/(v+v²) = 1/v − 1/(1+v) has antiderivative log(v/(1+v)) we get ∫1∞ dv/(v+v²) = log 2 but ∫01 dv/(v+v²) = ∞. So (b) holds and (a) fails in isolation.

When T∗ and S∗ are both finite, the same classical mechanism applied to f̃(y) = f(−y) in reversed time gives a two-parameter family: for (S,T) ∈ [0,∞]² there is a solution whose zero set is [−S, T], defined on (−S − S∗, T + T∗), with the convention that an infinite parameter removes the corresponding bound. These are the maximal solutions in the standard sense, and x ≡ 0 is the only one defined on all of ℝ.

The point relevant to §5 falls out. For every S, the restriction to [0,∞) of the solution indexed by (S,T) is the same xT — the zero set meets [0,∞) in [0,T] whatever S is. So each forward-maximal solution extends backward in continuum-many ways, and none of them is maximal.

BAppendix · A mechanical realisation

It is natural to ask whether a Newtonian particle can exhibit the mechanism. It can, though not in the way one first tries.

B.1 Not by steepening a dome

Norton’s construction [11] places a particle on a frictionless surface of revolution, with r the arc length along a meridian from the apex and h(r) the depth below it, giving r̈ = g h′(r). The obvious candidate here, h(r) = (1/g)(⅔r3/2 + ⅓r³), does give r̈ = √r + r², but it cannot be realised: parametrising the meridian by arc length forces ρ′² + z′² = 1 and hence |h′| ≤ 1, so |r̈| ≤ g, so r grows at most quadratically and cannot reach +∞ in finite time. With g = 9.8 the candidate already violates the constraint at r ≈ 2.85. The bound uses the whole Norton-type model — frictionless, uniform gravity, arc-length meridional coordinate, purely meridional motion with zero angular momentum, and the particle remaining on the surface; with angular momentum the equation gains a centrifugal term (pθ²/m²)·ρ′(r)/ρ(r)³, unbounded as ρ → 0. So this closes one route and no more: §B.2 gives a mechanical system that does escape.

B.2 In a potential

Let V(x) = −(⅔x3/2 + ⅓x³) for x ≥ 0, so ẍ = −V′(x) = √x + x², with x(0) = ẋ(0) = 0. Energy conservation from rest at the origin gives ½ẋ² = −V(x), that is ẋ = G(x) with

G(x) = (4/3·x3/2 + 2/3·x³)1/2,

and conversely G G′ = d/dx(½G²) = √x + x², so solutions of the first-order equation solve the second-order one. The even extension Ĝ(x) = G(|x|) is continuous with Ĝ(0) = 0 and Ĝ > 0 off the origin; G(x) ≍ x3/4 as x → 0+ and ≍ x3/2 as x → ∞, so both integrals converge. Everything in §3 and §4 therefore applies, with

T∗mech = ∫0∞ dx/G(x) = (2/3)·√(3/2)·2−1/3·B(⅙, ⅓) ≈ 5.4521363617.
What this realises

The mechanism of §4, in a Newtonian system — not the example of §2. The reduced right-hand side G is not |x|2/3 + x², and accordingly T∗mech ≠ T∗: a different member of the same class. Two caveats. The potential is unbounded below, the idealisation that attracts the standard objection to escape solutions [7]; and V is C1 but not C2 at the origin, the force being continuous and not locally Lipschitz there. Whether such a force law counts as a legitimate Newtonian system is the question the dome literature asks about √r [9, 11]; we take no position.
