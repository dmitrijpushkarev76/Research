One History or Many?

Whether a complete physical state and the laws of nature fix exactly one possible future — and what, if anything, that settles about alternative possibilities.

Scope. Mathematics, mathematical physics, theoretical physics, quantum foundations, philosophy of physics, formal logic. No neuroscience, psychology, or cognitive science.
Machine verification. 27 theorems in Lean 4.24.0 (core only, no Mathlib, no sorry); 13 symbolic checks in SymPy 1.14.0. Both reproducible from the accompanying source.

Abstract

This report investigates whether the mathematical structure of physical law implies that a complete state St together with the laws L admits exactly one physically possible continuation. The central object is ℋ(St, L), the set of histories compatible with that state and those laws; the thesis under test is |ℋ(St, L)| = 1.

Four findings organise the result. First, the inference the thesis is usually taken to license — from uniqueness to the absence of alternatives — is a definitional unfolding with no physical content; it is proved here in three lines of Lean and depends on no axioms whatever. Every substantive question lies in the antecedent. Second, |ℋ| = 1 factors into existence and uniqueness, and physics fails the existence half far more often than the uniqueness half: finite-time blow-up, non-collision singularities and geodesic incompleteness yield |ℋ| = 0, not |ℋ| > 1. A theory can be deterministic and admit no complete history at all. Third, determinism is not a property of a world but of a world under a description: coarsening the state space manufactures it and refining destroys it, so the question is ill-posed until the individuation of states is fixed — the abstract shape of the hole argument. Fourth, determinism does not require an evolution operator; a law can be a global constraint that fixes exactly one history while admitting no step function at all.

On the physics: uniqueness is conditional in Newtonian and Hamiltonian mechanics, holds in general relativity only within the maximal globally hyperbolic development and only up to isometry, and divides the quantum interpretations sharply — Bohmian mechanics and Everettian quantum mechanics are deterministic, objective-collapse theories are not, and Bell's theorem constrains none of this. The best-supported hypothesis is conditional determinism: several major theories are deterministic, and current physics does not establish universal determinism.

On the philosophical conclusion: the step from |ℋ| = 1 to "no alternative action" is valid only given the supervenience of action on physical history, which is a separate and substantive premise, exhibited here as an explicit countermodel. Given that premise, leeway-libertarian free will is incompatible with the thesis — but analytically, not as a discovery about physics. Indeterminism does not rescue it either.

Verdicts

A · Mathematical determinismIs the uniqueness proposition mathematically valid?
Established

Valid, and trivially so — machine-checked, zero axioms. The non-trivial mathematics is elsewhere: in the conditions (Lipschitz continuity, completeness of the flow, global hyperbolicity) under which uniqueness obtains at all.

B · Physical determinismDoes current fundamental physics justify applying it to the universe?
Unsupported

Not false — not established. There is no complete fundamental theory; the notion of a complete instantaneous state of the universe is not well-defined in general relativity and arguably absent in canonical quantum gravity; and the measurement problem is open, with the leading candidate theories divided on determinism.

C · Unique physical historyDoes a complete state plus laws imply exactly one history?
Conditional

True in Newtonian mechanics restricted to Lipschitz forces with complete flows; in general relativity only up to isometry and only inside the maximal globally hyperbolic development; at the fundamental level in Bohmian and Everettian quantum mechanics; false in objective-collapse theories and in textbook collapse quantum mechanics.

D · Alternative possibilitiesCan a different outcome occur with identical prior state and laws?
Conditional

No — given |ℋ| = 1, for a fixed individuation and a fixed level of description. All three qualifications are load-bearing, and the antecedent is not established for our universe.

E · Libertarian free willDoes the answer to D conflict with it?
Established (conditional)

The conditional holds and is analytic: if libertarian free will requires alternative possibilities under an identical past and identical laws, |ℋ| = 1 excludes it in one line. The unconditional claim that physics refutes libertarian free will is unsupported. Separately, and strongly supported: indeterminism does not rescue it — randomness supplies no control.

1Research Question

The question is not whether the future can be predicted. It is whether the future is uniquely determined as a physical possibility. These come apart, and most confusion in this area comes from running them together.

Four propositions must be held apart throughout. They are routinely treated as one.

The four propositions DERIVED

P1 — Causal dependence. The future state depends on the prior state: not every state permits the same continuations.

P2 — Deterministic evolution. The prior complete state uniquely determines the future state.

P3 — Unique history. The prior complete state and the laws permit exactly one complete history: |ℋ(St, L)| = 1.

P4 — Predictability. An observer can calculate the future.

P1 does not imply P2. A law may constrain the future without fixing it. The coin countermodel of §20 is a law under which the state at time 0 genuinely restricts what can follow — so dependence is real — while leaving two continuations open. Dependence is a matter of the map from states to sets of continuations being non-constant; determinism is a matter of those sets being singletons. MACHINE

P2 does not imply P4. The evolution operator in the formalization of §19 is an arbitrary function F : S → S, quantified over with no computability restriction whatever. A law of the form h(n+1) = F(h(n)) is deterministic for every F, computable or not. Determinism is a modal property of the space of possible histories; predictability is an epistemic and computational property of agents embedded in one. Nothing about the first entails anything about the second. MACHINE

P2 does not imply P3 either, and this is the finding of §4 that most treatments miss. P2 as usually stated delivers at most one continuation. P3 asserts exactly one. The difference is existence, and existence fails in ordinary physical models.

2Definitions

The following are the official definitions used throughout, and the ones formalized in §19. They are chosen to be as weak as possible, so that the thesis is not smuggled into them.

Definition 1 · History

Given a time set T and a state space S, a history is a total function h : T → S. The brief's H>t is the restriction of a history to {t′ ∈ T : t′ > t}.

Definition 2 · Law

A law L is a predicate on histories: it says which total histories are physically possible. Nothing more.

This is deliberate. Defining a law as a differential equation, a transition function, or an evolution operator would presuppose the initial-value paradigm and thereby build a large part of the thesis into the vocabulary. Under Definition 2, global-constraint or "all-at-once" laws are in scope, as are laws with no distinguished temporal direction. §19 exhibits a law that fixes exactly one history and admits no evolution operator at all. MACHINE

Definition 3 · The central object

ℋ(St, L)  =  { h : L(h)  and  h(t) = St }

The set of possible histories passing through state St at time t. The thesis under test is |ℋ(St, L)| = 1. Working with this set, rather than with the slogan "the future is determined", is what makes the rest of the analysis tractable.

Definition 4 · Determinism

L is deterministic iff any two possible histories that agree at even one instant agree at every instant:

∀ h₁ h₂ . L(h₁) ∧ L(h₂) ∧ (∃t . h₁(t) = h₂(t)) ⟹ h₁ = h₂

This is the Montague–Lewis possible-worlds form: no two nomologically possible worlds agree at a time and diverge later. Note carefully what it does not say. It does not say that any history exists.

3Mathematical Definition of Determinism

Definition 4 and the set-theoretic formulation are equivalent, which licenses moving between them freely.

Theorem 1 MACHINE

L is deterministic iff for every time t and state s, the set ℋ(s, L) at t has at most one element.

deterministic_iff_atMostOne — depends on no axioms.

Three consequences of stating it this way, each of which cuts against a common informal claim.

Determinism is description-relative. Definition 4 quantifies over a state space. Change the state space and the verdict changes. If the states are coarsened until all are identified, every law becomes deterministic — vacuously, since all histories are then equal. Refine the description and determinism can be destroyed. So "is the universe deterministic?" is not a well-formed question until the individuation of states is fixed, and fixing it is a substantive commitment, not bookkeeping. This is the abstract skeleton of the hole argument (§7). MACHINE

Determinism is time-symmetric as stated, and needn't be. Definition 4 requires agreement in both temporal directions. Forward-only determinism is strictly weaker: §20 exhibits an irreversible law that is future-deterministic and not deterministic. Whenever a text says "the past determines the future", check which of the two it has established. MACHINE

Determinism does not require an evolution operator. Having one is sufficient for forward determinism, and not necessary for determinism. A law can fix exactly one history globally while no function on instantaneous states generates it — because the instantaneous state need not carry enough information to fix its own successor. §19 gives such a law explicitly. Formulations of determinism in terms of S(t) → S(t+Δt) are therefore strictly narrower than determinism itself, and the narrowing is not innocent: it presupposes the initial-value paradigm. Adlam has argued at length that this presupposition should be dropped and determinism generalised to constraint-based laws — her §1.1 takes the Wheeler–DeWitt constraint and the Lagrangian/path-integral "all-at-once" laws as the cases that force the move (Determinism Beyond Time Evolution, arXiv:2110.07656v3, §1.1, pp. 3–4). 4Existence and Uniqueness

This is the section the brief correctly identifies as central, and it yields the report's first non-obvious result.

4.1 The classical apparatus

For an initial-value problem dx/dt = f(x, t), x(t₀) = x₀:

  • Peano gives existence of a local solution from continuity of f alone. It gives no uniqueness.
  • Picard–Lindelöf gives local existence and uniqueness when f is continuous in t and Lipschitz in x.
  • Neither gives global existence. Solutions can leave every compact set in finite time.

So the necessary and sufficient conditions the brief asks for do not take the form of a single property. Uniqueness of the solution through each point is what makes a theory deterministic; local Lipschitz continuity is the standard sufficient condition, and it is not necessary — dx/dt = −x1/3 has unique solutions despite failing Lipschitz at the origin, because the flow is directed into the singular point rather than out of it. The honest statement is: determinism is uniqueness of solutions through each state; Lipschitz continuity is a convenient sufficient condition for it, satisfied by textbook models and violated by constructible ones.

4.2 Uniqueness is not existence, and physics fails existence more often

|ℋ| = 1 is a conjunction: at least one and at most one. Determinism as standardly defined delivers only the second conjunct.

Theorem 2 · Vacuous determinism MACHINE

The law admitting no possible history is deterministic and satisfies |ℋ| ≤ 1, yet |ℋ| = 1 is false of it. Uniqueness without existence is not "one possible future"; it is no possible future.

vacuous_deterministic, vacuous_atMostOne, vacuous_not_exactlyOne.

This is not a logician's curiosity. The failure mode it names is pervasive:

dx/dt = x²,  x(0) = 1  ⟹  x(t) = 1/(1 − t),  x(t) → ∞ as t → 1⁻ verified symbolically · unique solution, no total history

The solution is unique wherever it exists, and it does not exist on all of ℝ. If a history is required to be a total function of time — as Definition 1 requires, and as the brief's H>t requires — then ℋ is empty, and |ℋ| = 1 is false. Not because the future branches. Because it runs out. MACHINE

The same structure recurs at every level of physics: incomplete Hamiltonian flows, non-collision singularities in the n-body problem (§5), geodesic incompleteness and the singularity theorems in general relativity (§7). Each is a failure of existence, and each falsifies |ℋ| = 1 without producing a single alternative history. Any argument that moves from "the laws are deterministic" to "there is exactly one possible future" has skipped this conjunct.

4.3 Discrete versus continuous time

The brief asks whether "every next moment is determined by the previous moment" is even well-defined. In continuous time it is not: there is no next moment. The claim must be reformulated either as a differential condition or as the global condition S(t₀) → S(t) for all later t, and these are not equivalent.

In discrete time, local uniqueness does give global forward uniqueness, by induction on the number of steps. MACHINE In continuous time it does not, and the Norton dome (§5) is exactly a counterexample: the equation of motion has a well-defined right-hand side everywhere, yet solutions bifurcate. The passage from local to global is the substantive step, and it is where continuous-time physics can fail.

5Classical Mechanics

Classical mechanics is the textbook home of determinism and does not deserve the reputation.

5.1 The favourable case

For a finite system of point particles interacting through smooth, bounded forces, the equations of motion are Lipschitz on the relevant domain, Picard–Lindelöf applies, and trajectories through each phase point are unique. In Hamiltonian form the state is a point in phase space (q, p) ∈ M, evolution is the flow of the Hamiltonian vector field XH, and where that flow is complete it is a one-parameter group of symplectomorphisms — uniqueness in both time directions, with Liouville's theorem giving conservation of phase volume. This is the setting in which Laplace's demon is well-posed.

It is worth being precise about what Laplace's demon actually establishes, because it is an epistemic image doing modal work. The demon is a claim about P4 given P2 — if evolution is unique and the state is knowable to arbitrary precision and the computation is performable, the future is calculable. The modal thesis P3 needs none of these. Deleting the demon changes nothing about whether |ℋ| = 1.

5.2 The n-body system, scaled up

The brief's motivating example — a closed system of objects with different masses, velocities, positions, materials, and electromagnetic and gravitational interactions — is instructive precisely because scaling it changes nothing modal and everything epistemic.

At n = 10, with regular interactions, the solution through each phase point is unique. At n = 1023 it is still unique: the dimension of phase space has grown, the Lipschitz argument has not weakened. What changes is that no closed-form solution exists (already true at n = 3), that error growth becomes catastrophic (§6), and that the state becomes unknowable in practice. Every one of those is P4. Complexity does not manufacture alternative futures; it makes a unique future incalculable. This is the single most reliable result in the whole investigation, and it is the answer to the brief's question in §8. DERIVED

What does change with scale is the availability of the pathologies below, which need either singular potentials or unboundedly many degrees of freedom.

5.3 Norton's dome

Norton's example is the cleanest known failure of uniqueness inside Newtonian mechanics. A mass slides frictionlessly on a dome whose height below the apex, as a function of arc length r from the apex, is

h(r) = (2/3g) · r3/2  ⟹  tangential force per unit mass = g · dh/dr = √r  ⟹  r̈ = √r verified symbolically — the factor 2/3g is chosen exactly to cancel g

With the mass initially at rest at the apex, r(0) = ṙ(0) = 0, this admits the trivial solution r(t) = 0 and, for every T ≥ 0, the one-parameter family

r(t) = (t − T)⁴ / 144  for t ≥ T,   r(t) = 0  for t < T verified: r̈ = (t−T)²/12 = √r, and r(T) = ṙ(T) = r̈(T) = 0, so the join is C²

The mass, at rest at the apex, spontaneously begins to move at an arbitrary time T, with no cause and no violation of the equation of motion. The mechanism is the failure of Lipschitz continuity of √r at r = 0: the difference quotient √r / r diverges as r → 0⁺. All of this was checked symbolically for this report rather than taken on authority. MACHINE

The adversarial case. Whether this shows Newtonian mechanics is indeterministic, or shows that the dome is not a Newtonian system, is contested. Norton presses the first, and answers the standard objections head-on: his abstract states that the indeterminism has been discounted on the grounds that it "draws on an incomplete rendering of Newtonian physics; or it is 'unphysical'; or it employs illicit idealizations," and that he analyses and rejects each. 5.4 Escape to infinity and space invaders

The second family of failures needs no exotic surface, only gravity.

Xia proved the existence of non-collision singularities in Newtonian systems — a five-body configuration in which particles are driven to spatial infinity in finite time (Annals of Mathematics 135(3), 1992, 411–468). It is not known whether one can have a pure gravitational space invader system with exactly four particles. But Jeff Xia proved in 1988 that one can have such a system with n particles if n ≥ 5" (Philosophy of Science 75, 2008, 803). SOURCED The remaining case fell later: Xue's "Noncollision singularities in a planar 4-body problem" (Acta Mathematica 224, 2020, 253–388) closes n = 4, and with it the conjecture. Mather and McGehee had earlier obtained unbounded-in-finite-time behaviour in the collinear four-body problem (1975) — but by way of infinitely many binary collisions, so it is not a non-collision singularity. BIBLIO

Two distinct morals, and they are usually conflated:

  • Forwards, this is a failure of existence: the solution has no continuation past the singular time, so ℋ is empty, exactly the Theorem 2 situation.
  • Backwards — since Newtonian mechanics is time-reversal invariant — it is the "space invaders" scenario: particles arrive from spatial infinity at an arbitrary time, unheralded by any prior state. Earman's A Primer on Determinism (Reidel, 1986) is the standard source for the philosophical use of this; the Stanford Encyclopedia's Causal Determinism (Hoefer, rev. 2023, §4.1) gives the argument in the same form used here — "Clearly, a world with a space invader does fail to be deterministic. Before t = t*, there was nothing in the state of things to enable the prediction of the appearance of the invader" — and credits the enabling mechanism to Xia (1992). SOURCED This is a genuine failure of uniqueness, and unlike the dome it needs no non-Lipschitz force — only the non-compactness of space and the unboundedness of the gravitational potential.

Space invaders are the more serious case, because the standard reply to the dome (the model is unphysical) is harder to run against a system of four point masses under ordinary gravity.

6Chaos and Predictability

Deterministic chaos is the most frequently misused evidence in this debate. It is evidence for the opposite of what it is usually taken to show.

A chaotic system exhibits sensitive dependence on initial conditions: nearby trajectories separate at a rate governed by a positive Lyapunov exponent, δ(t) ≈ δ₀eλt. Every trajectory in such a system is nonetheless unique. Separation is a statement about distinct initial conditions diverging; non-uniqueness would be a statement about one initial condition admitting two futures. These are different claims about different objects, and the first is compatible with — indeed presupposes — the second's failure. MACHINE

The distinction, stated once

Unpredictability is a relation between an agent, its measurement precision, its computational resources, and a system. Non-uniqueness is a property of the solution set of the laws. Chaos maximises the first and leaves the second untouched. Norton's dome does the reverse: it is perfectly predictable in the trivial solution and non-unique.

There is, however, a genuinely unsettling result in this vicinity that deserves more attention than chaos does. A body of ergodic theory from the 1960s onward, surveyed by Ornstein and Weiss (Bulletin of the AMS 24(1), 1991, 11–116), established that a wide class of deterministic measure-preserving systems arising from classical dynamics are measure-theoretically isomorphic to Bernoulli shifts — hard-sphere systems, billiards with convex obstacles, geodesic flows of negative curvature, and dissipative cases such as the logistic, Hénon and discrete Lorenz maps. Werndl has developed the philosophical consequence, arguing that certain deterministic and indeterministic descriptions are observationally equivalent: her result is that "there are several deterministic systems used in science which give the same predictions at every observation level as Markov processes," where an observation level is a finite-resolution partition of the state space (Studies in History and Philosophy of Modern Physics 40, 2009, 232–242; arXiv:1310.1615, abstract and §5). SOURCED

If that is right, then determinism is not merely hard to detect; for a significant class of systems it is empirically undetectable in principle from the statistics of finite-resolution observations. This matters for Verdict B: it means the question "is the actual world deterministic?" may be underdetermined by all possible finite-precision evidence, not just by the evidence we happen to have. A separate line of attack — Del Santo and Gisin's "Physics without determinism: alternative interpretations of classical physics" (Physical Review A 100, 2019, 062107), and Landsman's work on undecidability and indeterminism — presses the complementary point that classical determinism presupposes what they name, in those words, the principle of infinite precision: "the tacit, metaphysical assumption that there exists an actual value of every physical quantity, with its infinite predetermined digits," which "translates into the attribution of a physical meaning to mathematical real numbers" and which they argue is an unphysical idealisation (abstract). SOURCED

7Relativity and Spacetime

7.1 The initial-value formulation

General relativity has a genuine initial-value formulation, and it is the strongest determinism result in physics — with three qualifications, each of which matters.

Initial data is a triple (Σ, hab, Kab): a three-manifold with a Riemannian metric and a symmetric tensor (the extrinsic curvature), subject to the Hamiltonian and momentum constraint equations. Choquet-Bruhat and Geroch ("Global aspects of the Cauchy problem in general relativity", Communications in Mathematical Physics 14(4), 1969, 329–335) established that data satisfying the constraints admits a development that is maximal, and that it is unique up to isometry. Two qualifications belong inside the statement rather than after it. The theorem is for the vacuum Einstein equations; and the maximal development extends every other globally hyperbolic development — not every development — where a globally hyperbolic development is one into which the initial data embeds as a Cauchy surface. In Sbierski's rendering of their result: "Given initial data there exists a GHD M̃ that is an extension of any other GHD of the same initial data. The GHD M̃ is unique up to isometry and is called the maximal globally hyperbolic development (MGHD)" (arXiv:1309.7591, Thm. 2.8). SOURCED The restriction to globally hyperbolic developments is not a technicality: as Sbierski puts it, "by looking at the Kerr solution for example… one can only hope to obtain a global uniqueness result if one restricts consideration to globally hyperbolic developments" (§1.1). That restriction is exactly what strong cosmic censorship, in §7.2, is trying to make harmless.

The qualifications:

  1. Uniqueness is only up to isometry. The theorem does not deliver a unique spacetime; it delivers a unique isometry class. Whether that counts as determinism is the hole-argument question (§7.3).
  2. It holds only within the maximal globally hyperbolic development. Global hyperbolicity is precisely the condition that a Cauchy surface exists — i.e. determinism is not derived, it is assumed as a restriction on which spacetimes are considered.
  3. The initial data is not free. The constraint equations mean the "state on a slice" is not an arbitrary specification; it is already a global condition on the slice. This anticipates §8.

7.2 Where general relativity is genuinely indeterministic

Beyond Cauchy horizons. In the Kerr and Reissner–Nordström interiors, the maximal globally hyperbolic development ends at a Cauchy horizon past which the metric can be extended in more than one way — the field equations no longer fix the continuation. Strong cosmic censorship is the conjecture that this is non-generic. Its status is now known to be delicate: Dafermos and Luk's "The interior of dynamical vacuum black holes I: The C⁰-stability of the Kerr Cauchy horizon" (arXiv:1710.01722; Annals of Mathematics 202(2), September 2025, 309–630) proves that for data on a spacelike hypersurface already inside the black hole, representing the expected geometry of a dynamical black hole settling down to Kerr, the maximal future development has a non-trivial Cauchy horizon across which the metric is continuously extendible (Thm. 1). Their own summary is that "these theorems together with a positive resolution of Conjecture 1 imply that Conjecture 2 is in fact false" (§1, §1.3.3) — so the falsification is conditional on the Kerr stability conjecture and on the sequel. That revision (their Conjecture 3) asks for inextendibility "as a Lorentzian manifold with continuous metric and Christoffel symbols locally square integrable" — a weaker inextendibility demand than C⁰, which is why it can survive Thm. 1. Closed timelike curves. Solutions such as Gödel's (Reviews of Modern Physics 21(3), 1949, 447–450) admit CTCs and no global Cauchy surface, so the initial-value formulation does not apply at all. The Stanford Encyclopedia's Time Machines entry puts the relevant fact sharply: "through every point in this spacetime there is a CTC" — and a spacetime in which every point lies on a closed timelike curve can have no Cauchy surface, since a Cauchy surface must meet each inextendible causal curve exactly once. Singularities. Geodesic incompleteness means histories that cannot be continued — again the Theorem 2 failure of existence rather than a proliferation of futures.

7.3 The hole argument, and why it is a determinism problem

Take a solution, choose a compact region ("the hole"), and apply a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside it and non-trivial inside. Because Einstein's equations are generally covariant, the result is another solution, agreeing with the first everywhere outside the hole and differing within. If those count as distinct physical possibilities, general relativity is indeterministic — and trivially, catastrophically so. The standard resolution is to deny that they are distinct: the physical state is the isometry class, not the metric-on-a-manifold. Earman and Norton's 1987 presentation (British Journal for the Philosophy of Science 38(4), 515–525) is the locus classicus — though the credit for recognising the argument's non-trivial character belongs to Stachel (1980); as the Stanford Encyclopedia's Hole Argument entry puts it, "In Earman and Norton (1987), the argument was recast as one that explicitly targets spacetime substantivalism." SOURCED

This is precisely the description-relativity result of §3 appearing in a real theory: whether general relativity is deterministic depends on how states are individuated, and the formalism does not settle it. The debate remains active — Halvorson and Manchak's "Closing the Hole Argument" (BJPS 76(2), 2025, 295–318) argues that hole isometries are unique in a sense that removes the argument's basis — in the Stanford Encyclopedia's summary, "because there is a unique metric-preserving map ('isometry') relating two solutions of general relativity related by the hole transformation, the hole argument is thereby blocked." SOURCED Manchak, Barrett, Halvorson and Weatherall's "Determinism and Asymmetry in General Relativity" (arXiv:2503.05668v2; BJPS, 2025) starts from three definitions of increasing strength due to Belot (1995), of which "the strongest… is particularly interesting for spacetime theories because it involves an asymmetry condition called 'rigidity'," and then adds further, stronger asymmetry conditions yielding stronger forms of determinism (abstract). SOURCED That a paper of 2025 is still fixing the definition of determinism for general relativity is the relevant datum for Verdict B.

7.4 Block universe, eternalism, determinism — three different things

The brief requires these be kept apart, and they must be, because conflating them produces a fallacy that appears constantly in popular treatments.

  • Determinism: the complete state and laws uniquely constrain the rest of history. A modal claim about the size of ℋ.
  • Eternalism: past, present and future events are equally real. An ontological claim about what exists.
  • Block universe: reality is represented as a four-dimensional spacetime structure. A representational claim.

Determinism does not imply eternalism. A presentist can hold that only the present is real while the laws fix a unique successor; nothing in Definition 4 quantifies over existing future events. Eternalism does not imply determinism. This is the important direction, and the decisive case is objective-collapse theory: a GRW world has a perfectly good four-dimensional history — one block, fully real, every event laid out — in which the transitions were stochastic and the state at t did not fix what followed. A block universe is a record of what happened; determinism is a claim about what could have. A block can be a block without being the only possible one. DERIVED

So a "fixed future" in the sense of eternalism does not require determinism, and determinism does not require a block universe. The brief's instruction not to assume the block universe settles the question is correct.

8The Complete State Problem

This is, as the brief anticipates, the deepest difficulty in the entire investigation — and it attacks Premise 1 rather than the inference.

The thesis presupposes that "the complete physical state of the universe at time t" denotes something. In classical mechanics it does: a point in phase space. Past that, the notion degrades at every step.

8.1 There is no preferred t

Special relativity already removes absolute simultaneity: "the state of the universe at t" is frame-relative, and different foliations give different states. General relativity worsens this — a generic spacetime admits no preferred foliation, many admit no global Cauchy surface at all, and in a spacetime with closed timelike curves the notion of a global instantaneous state is not merely unnatural but unavailable. The initial-value formulation of §7.1 works by restricting attention to globally hyperbolic spacetimes, which is to say: by assuming a well-posed notion of "the state at a time" exists rather than showing that it does.

8.2 The state is not freely specifiable

The Hamiltonian and momentum constraints mean that initial data on a slice is not an unconstrained list of local values; it satisfies elliptic equations relating values across the whole slice. Gauge redundancy compounds this: the mathematical object is not the state, the equivalence class is. A "complete state" is thus already a global, quotiented object — not the intuitive picture of a complete list of local facts at an instant.

8.3 In canonical quantum gravity there may be no state-at-a-time at all

Quantising the constraints yields the Wheeler–DeWitt equation, Ĥ Ψ = 0. There is no time parameter in it. This is the "problem of time" — the frozen-formalism problem — and it is not a technicality of one approach: it follows from the Hamiltonian's being a constraint that annihilates physical states, which follows from general covariance. Isham's "Canonical Quantum Gravity and the Problem of Time" (arXiv:gr-qc/9210011), Kuchař's "Time and Interpretations of Quantum Gravity" (International Journal of Modern Physics D 20(supp01), 2011, 3–86; first circulated 1992) and Anderson's "The Problem of Time in Quantum Gravity" (arXiv:1009.2157) are the standard references for the taxonomy of the problem and the proposed resolutions. BIBLIO

If the fundamental description of the universe has no time evolution, then the question "given the complete state at t, how many continuations are there?" does not have a false answer. It has no answer, because it does not parse.

The consequence for the thesis

Premise 1 of the core argument (§18) is not established, and may not be well-formed at the fundamental level. This is the strongest single objection to the whole project, and it does not come from quantum indeterminacy — it comes from general covariance.

Can the thesis be reformulated without it? Yes, and this is where the constraint-based conception of law earns its keep. Replace "the state at t plus the laws fix the rest" with "the laws admit exactly one total history", i.e. |{h : L(h)}| = 1. This is well-defined with no notion of instantaneous state, no foliation, and no evolution operator; §19 exhibits exactly such a law. The reformulated thesis is stronger than the original (it is Adlam's strong holistic determinism — Def. 3.2, "a world satisfies strong holistic determinism iff it satisfies holistic determinism and there is only one mosaic in the intersection of the set of constraints associated with its laws of nature" — and it is what Wheeler–DeWitt-style laws would have to satisfy to be deterministic at all. Two notes on the label. The bare term "strong determinism" is Penrose's, which Adlam cites and distinguishes her own scheme from; and her definition carries a second conjunct this reformulation does not, namely that the laws induce no non-trivial probability distributions over constraints, ruling out chance as well as multiplicity), and it is the version that survives §8. It is also, notably, further from anything current physics establishes.

9Quantum Mechanics

The claim "quantum mechanics proves the universe is indeterministic" is the single most common error in this area. The correct statement is narrower and more interesting.

9.1 The formalism has two dynamical rules, and only one is stochastic

Textbook quantum mechanics contains:

  • Unitary evolution. iħ ∂tΨ = ĤΨ. Linear, deterministic, time-reversible, and — for self-adjoint Ĥ, by Stone's theorem — generating a one-parameter unitary group. Given Ψ(t₀), Ψ(t) is unique for all t. Considered by itself, the Schrödinger equation is more robustly deterministic than Newton's laws: it is linear, so none of the §5 pathologies arise.
  • Collapse on measurement, with outcome probabilities given by the Born rule. Stochastic, non-linear, irreversible.

All the indeterminism sits in the second rule. And the second rule is precisely the part of the formalism that no one regards as fundamental, because it is stated in terms of "measurement" — an undefined primitive that appears in no other fundamental physical law. That is the measurement problem. DERIVED

The correct inference. Quantum mechanics as a formalism does not settle determinism, because the formalism is inconsistent as it stands: it contains two incompatible dynamical rules with no principled account of when each applies. The determinism question is therefore displaced onto the question of how the measurement problem is solved — and the candidate solutions differ on the answer. Determinism is downstream of the measurement problem, not upstream of it.

9.2 Decoherence does not settle it

Decoherence explains why interference between macroscopically distinct branches becomes practically unobservable, through entanglement with environmental degrees of freedom. It is a consequence of unitary evolution — hence deterministic — and it does not produce a single outcome from a superposition. It explains why we do not see interference; it does not explain why we see one result. Decoherence is therefore neutral on determinism, and reading it as a solution to the measurement problem is a category error. DERIVED

9.3 What is the "state", and is it complete?

Whether Ψ is the complete state is itself contested. The Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem constrains ψ-epistemic models — models on which the quantum state represents mere information about an underlying physical state — showing that under an assumption of preparation independence such models conflict with quantum predictions. Their own statement: "any model in which a quantum state represents mere information about an underlying physical state of the system, and in which systems that are prepared independently have independent physical states, must make predictions which contradict those of quantum theory" (Nature Physics 8(6), 2012, 475–478; arXiv:1111.3328, abstract). SOURCED The relevance here is that "complete state" in Premise 1 cannot be read off the formalism; it is fixed by the interpretation, which is what §§11–14 are about.

10Bell's Theorem

The brief asks what Bell's theorem actually rules out. The short answer: it does not rule out determinism, and it never did.

10.1 What is assumed and what is derived

A crucial point of history that is almost universally reversed in secondary accounts: in the 1964 argument, determinism is not an assumption but a conclusion. Bell takes the EPR argument — locality plus the perfect anti-correlations predicted for the singlet state — to establish that the outcomes are predetermined, and then shows that predetermined local values cannot reproduce the quantum statistics for other measurement angles. The Stanford Encyclopedia's Bell's Theorem puts the point in exactly this direction: from the perfect anticorrelation "it follows that the result of any such measurement must actually be predetermined (Bell 1964, 195)… This suggests that OD [outcome determinism] is not being assumed, but, rather, derived, via an EPR-type argument, from a locality assumption and the perfect anticorrelations predicted by the considered quantum state." SOURCED Bell's 1976 "The theory of local beables" replaces this with a condition of local causality formulated directly in spacetime terms, from which factorizability is derived rather than posited — "In Bell (1976) and (1990), Bell derives the factorizability condition from a condition he calls the Principle of Local Causality" — and Bell insisted on the direction of derivation: "Very often such factorizability is taken as the starting point of the analysis. Here we have preferred to see it not as the formulation of 'local causality', but as a consequence thereof." SOURCED One correction to the usual potted history, this report's first draft included: dropping outcome determinism was not first achieved in 1976. Bell (1971) had already given a proof of the CHSH inequality "that relies on neither the assumption of perfect anticorrelation nor an assumption of outcome determinism," so coverage of stochastic local theories dates from there; what 1976 adds is the spacetime formulation of local causality from which the condition follows. SOURCED

So the logical situation is the opposite of the folklore: determinism entered Bell's original reasoning as something derived from locality, and the mature version of the theorem does not need it as a premise at all. A theorem that does not assume determinism cannot refute it.

10.2 What the theorem actually constrains

The derivation requires, at minimum:

  • Local causality (factorizability): a complete specification of beables in the overlap of the backward light cones screens off correlations.
  • Statistical independence (measurement independence): the distribution of hidden variables is independent of the measurement settings.

Violation of the resulting inequality — confirmed experimentally, including in loophole-closing experiments — refutes the conjunction. It leaves each disjunct standing individually:

  • Deny local causality → Bohmian mechanics (deterministic, explicitly non-local).
  • Deny statistical independence → superdeterminism (deterministic).
  • Deny that measurements have single outcomes → Everett (deterministic).
  • Deny determinism → objective collapse (and note that this must also deny local causality, since collapse theories are non-local too).

Three of the four live responses to Bell are deterministic theories. The claim "Bell proved determinism is impossible" is not a defensible reading under any statement of the theorem. DERIVED

10.3 The Conway–Kochen free will theorem

Conway and Kochen's theorem is frequently cited as establishing indeterminism, and does not. From axioms SPIN, TWIN and FIN — replaced in the strong version by the single weaker axiom MIN — it concludes, verbatim: "The axioms SPIN, TWIN and MIN imply that the response of a spin 1 particle to a triple experiment is free — that is to say, is not a function of properties of that part of the universe that is earlier than this response with respect to any given inertial frame" (arXiv:0807.3286, §4). The antecedent is carried by MIN, which grants the experimenters free choice of settings; as the authors gloss it, "our theorem asserts that if experimenters have a certain freedom, then particles have exactly the same kind of freedom." SOURCED

This is a conditional whose antecedent is exactly the thesis under investigation. If |ℋ| = 1, the antecedent is false and the theorem is silent. Its actual content is a strengthening of Bell: no theory combining determinism with locality and measurement independence works. Read as an argument for indeterminism it is question-begging; read as a constraint on deterministic theories it is informative — they must be non-local or violate statistical independence. DERIVED

11Bohmian Mechanics

Bohmian mechanics is the decisive counterexample to "quantum mechanics entails indeterminism", which is why the brief is right to give it a section.

The complete state is a pair: the universal wavefunction Ψ and the actual particle configuration Q = (Q₁,…,QN). The dynamics is two equations, both deterministic:

iħ ∂tΨ = ĤΨ    (Schrödinger)
dQk/dt = (ħ/mk) · Im[ Ψ*∇kΨ / Ψ*Ψ ] (Q₁,…,QN)    (guiding equation) first-order in configuration — the state is (Ψ, Q), not (Ψ, Q, Q̇)

There is no collapse postulate and no stochastic element in the fundamental dynamics. Quantum randomness is epistemic: it arises because the initial configuration is unknown and distributed according to |Ψ|² — the quantum equilibrium hypothesis, developed by Dürr, Goldstein and Zanghì as a typicality claim about the universal configuration rather than a dynamical postulate. That is their own framing: "on the universal level, the physical significance of quantum equilibrium is as a measure of typicality, and the ultimate justification of the quantum equilibrium hypothesis is… in terms of the statistical behavior arising from a typical initial configuration" — where the measure of typicality is |Ψ₀|², singled out as "the only mathematically natural — because equivariant — candidate at hand" (Journal of Statistical Physics 67, 1992, 843–907, §4; arXiv:quant-ph/0308039). Given equilibrium, the theory reproduces the Born statistics exactly while remaining deterministic underneath. SOURCED

Does uniqueness hold globally? This is the sharp mathematical question, and the answer carries a real qualification. Berndl, Dürr, Goldstein, Peruzzi and Zanghì, "On the global existence of Bohmian mechanics" (Communications in Mathematical Physics 173(3), 1995, 647–673; arXiv:quant-ph/9503013), show that "for a large class of potentials the singularities of the velocity field and infinity will not be reached in finite time for typical initial values" (abstract) — i.e. global existence holds up to a set of measure zero in the equilibrium measure. The measure is exactly that: their P is the |ψ₀|² density, which "plays the role usually played by the 'equilibrium measure'" and "defines our notion of typicality" (§2), and the theorem is P(τ < ∞) = 0 (Thm. 3.1, Cor. 3.2). The potential class is wide enough to matter: H₀-form-bounded potentials with arbitrarily small relative bound, which covers power laws 1/rα with α < 2 — hence "the physically most relevant potential of N-particle Coulomb interaction with arbitrary charges and masses" — plus harmonic, anharmonic and arbitrarily strong repulsive potentials (§3). SOURCED One qualification the earlier draft of this report got wrong: the nodes of Ψ are not the only danger points. Berndl et al. are explicit that "in addition to the nodes of ψ, there are singularities comparable to those of Newtonian mechanics" — escape to infinity in finite time, and the singular points of the potential at the boundary of configuration space (§1). Which is to say the §5.4 failure mode recurs here, and the theorem rules out all three for typical data. So Bohmian determinism is not unconditional: it is determinism almost everywhere, which is the strongest statement available and is worth stating precisely rather than as a flat "yes".

The costs. The theory is explicitly non-local — the velocity of each particle depends instantaneously on the positions of all others — and this is not a defect but the required response to Bell. Its relativistic extension remains the principal open problem: "Bohmian mechanics is not Lorentz invariant. Nor can it easily be modified to accommodate Lorentz invariance," because configurations — simultaneous positions of all particles — are load-bearing in the formulation. Bell rated this the residual difficulty, saying the paradoxes "are simply disposed of by the 1952 theory of Bohm, leaving as the question, the question of Lorentz invariance." A foliation of spacetime is hard to avoid, but — a point worth keeping — it need not break Lorentz invariance if the foliation is itself dynamically determined by a Lorentz-invariant structure such as a time-like 4-vector field (Stanford Encyclopedia, Bohmian Mechanics, §14). SOURCED For the present investigation the relevant verdict is: a fully worked-out, empirically adequate, deterministic theory of non-relativistic quantum phenomena exists. Any argument from quantum mechanics to indeterminism must explain why this theory is not the true one — and no experiment currently distinguishes it.

12Everett / Many-Worlds

Everett is the case where the brief's question changes shape rather than getting an answer, and this is a substantive finding rather than an evasion.

The theory postulates exactly one dynamical law — the Schrödinger equation — applied universally, with no collapse. The universal wavefunction evolves deterministically and uniquely. Branching is not a further postulate; it is the emergence, via decoherence, of effectively non-interfering components of the universal state, each containing a copy of the experimenter recording a definite result.

Theorem 5 · Both answers are true at once MACHINE

A law can satisfy |ℋ| = 1 at the level of the universal state while the branch-relative histories are many and distinct. "Is there exactly one possible history?" is therefore not yet a question until the level of description is fixed.

everett_universal_unique, everett_branches_differ.

So, for the eight questions the brief poses of each interpretation: the dynamics is deterministic; the evolution of the fundamental state is unique; no stochasticity enters the fundamental dynamics; no collapse occurs; branching is involved; multiple outcomes are all physically realised; and whether the theory contains "one history or many" depends entirely on what "history" denotes — one universal history, many branch histories.

Are branches alternative possibilities? No — and this is the crux. A possibility is something that may or may not obtain. An Everettian branch does obtain; all of them do. The multiverse is not a space of possible worlds but a single actual world with a richer structure than it appears to have from inside a branch. Wilson's The Nature of Contingency: Quantum Physics as Modal Realism (Oxford, 2020) takes the opposite tack deliberately, developing an account on which Everettian branches are the possible worlds, so that quantum theory supplies the space of genuine possibilities rather than describing actuality alone. The book itself was not opened; the Stanford Encyclopedia's Many-Worlds Interpretation describes the framework, "indexicalism", as one "which involves a set of distinct diverging 'parallel' worlds in which each observer is located in only one world and all propositions are construed as self-locating," introduced to "vindicate treating weights as a candidate objective probability measure" — and notes the programme's success is contested. BIBLIO On that view Everett does deliver alternative possibilities — but by identifying possibility with branching, which is a metaphysical decision, not a result read off the physics.

The unresolved cost: probability. If every outcome occurs, what does a Born-rule probability mean? Deutsch and Wallace argue the Born weights are recoverable as the unique rational credences for an agent facing branching, via decision theory. The derivation is contested on two fronts, and the earlier draft of this report had the two rosters crossed. Following the Stanford Encyclopedia's Many-Worlds Interpretation: the early objection was circularity (Barnum et al. 2000; Baker 2007; Hemmo and Pitowsky 2007), and "as the program led to more explicit proofs, criticism turned to the decision-theoretic assumptions being made" (Lewis 2010; Albert 2010; Kent 2010; Price 2010). Zurek belongs on neither list — he offers a rival derivation of the Born rule from envariance, not an objection. Greaves and Myrvold pursue a third route, from empirical confirmation rather than decision theory ("Everett and Evidence", in Many Worlds?, Oxford, 2010, 264–304). SOURCED The determinism verdict does not depend on this dispute: Everett is deterministic either way. What depends on it is whether Everett is empirically adequate.

13Collapse Theories

Objective-collapse theories are the clearest case of genuine fundamental stochasticity, and the clearest test of what stochasticity is worth.

Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber ("Unified dynamics for microscopic and macroscopic systems", Physical Review D 34(2), 1986, 470–491) modify the dynamics so that the wavefunction evolves by the Schrödinger equation until a random time, at which it is multiplied by a narrow Gaussian centred on a random location, with the Born-rule distribution built into the jump law rather than triggered by "measurement". BIBLIO The rates are fixed so that microscopic systems collapse essentially never and macroscopic superpositions collapse essentially instantly, which dissolves the measurement problem by making the cut dynamical rather than definitional.

Here the answers to the brief's eight questions are unambiguous. The dynamics is stochastic; the evolution of the fundamental state is not unique; stochasticity enters the fundamental law; collapse is real; there is no branching; exactly one outcome is realised; there is one actual history and many possible ones. This is a theory on which |ℋ(St, L)| > 1 straightforwardly.

Theorem 8 · A unique chance function is not a unique history MACHINE

For any assignment of chances to histories whatsoever, non-uniqueness of the history persists. "The laws fix a unique probability measure over histories" does not entail "the laws fix a unique history".

unique_chance_does_not_give_unique_history.

This matters because it is tempting to say collapse theories are "deterministic at the level of the measure" — the Lindblad evolution of the density matrix is deterministic — and infer that nothing modal has changed. The inference fails. A deterministic law for the evolution of a probability distribution is compatible with many possible histories; it is a law about the ensemble, not the trajectory. MACHINE

Does stochasticity create genuine alternative possibilities? Yes — this is what fundamental indeterminism means, and it is the one case in which the answer to the brief's central question is a clean "more than one history is possible". Does that help with freedom? No, and §18 argues the point. An uncaused deviation is not an exercise of control by the agent; it is a deviation that happens to the agent. Randomness is not the missing ingredient in agency.

Collapse theories are also the only candidates on this list that are experimentally distinguishable from standard quantum mechanics in principle, since spontaneous localisation predicts small deviations from unitary evolution — a genuine empirical handle on the determinism question, currently constraining but not excluding the parameter space. The Stanford Encyclopedia's Collapse Theories confirms both halves: the models "involve phenomenological parameters which, if the theory is taken seriously, acquire the status of new constants of nature," and thanks to "remarkable improvements in the field of opto-mechanics and cold atoms, as well as nuclear physics… specific bounds have already been obtained for the parameters characterizing the theories under investigation" — bounds, not exclusion. SOURCED

14Superdeterminism

The brief asks that superdeterminism be taken seriously rather than dismissed. Taken seriously, it is coherent, deeply unattractive, and — importantly — not currently a worked-out physical theory.

The proposal denies statistical independence: the hidden-variable distribution is correlated with the measurement settings. Since statistical independence is a premise of Bell's derivation, denying it blocks the theorem and permits a local deterministic theory. Hossenfelder and Palmer's "Rethinking Superdeterminism" (Frontiers in Physics 8, 2020, article 139) argues that the standard dismissals are too quick — that "the existing objections to Superdeterminism are based on experience with classical physics and linear systems, but that this experience misleads us" — and that whether a violation of statistical independence is explanatorily acceptable cannot be settled without examining concrete models: "just by knowing a theory violates Statistical Independence one cannot tell anything about its explanatory power. For this one needs to study a concrete model." SOURCED 't Hooft's cellular-automaton interpretation (arXiv:1405.1548; Springer, Fundamental Theories of Physics 185, 2016) is the most developed programme in this direction. BIBLIO

The case against, stated at its strongest. Statistical independence is not an arbitrary technical assumption; it is what licenses controlled experiment in general. If the settings of an apparatus can be correlated with the properties of the system it measures, then randomised controlled experiment — not merely Bell tests — loses its warrant. The objection is not that superdeterminism is strange but that it is self-undermining as a scientific hypothesis, since the evidence for any theory is gathered by procedures whose validity it impugns.

The honest verdict. Superdeterminism is logically coherent, and it does provide a deterministic description compatible with the observed correlations. What does not currently exist is a superdeterministic model that reproduces the full quantum predictions across the range of experiments while making novel testable predictions. Until one does, superdeterminism is a research programme and a proof of the logical point that Bell does not refute determinism — not a theory that supports |ℋ| = 1. DERIVED

15Physical Possibility and Modal Logic

"Physically possible" must be pinned down, or the central question equivocates.

Five modalities, in decreasing permissiveness

Logical — consistent with the laws of logic. Mathematical — consistent with the relevant mathematics. Metaphysical — consistent with the essential natures of things. Nomological / physical — consistent with the actual laws of nature. Epistemic — consistent with what is known.

The thesis is about nomological possibility, indexed to a state. Writing ◇L,t,sA for "some possible history through state s at t satisfies A" and □L,t,sA for "every such history does":

Theorem 6 · Modal collapse MACHINE

If |ℋ(s, L)| = 1 then ◇L,t,sA ↔ □L,t,sA for every A: possibility and necessity coincide, and nothing about the future is contingent.

Uniqueness alone gives only ◇ → □. The converse needs existence: with no possible history at all, □ is vacuously true and ◇ is false. The full collapse is exactly |ℋ| = 1, not |ℋ| ≤ 1.

modal_collapse, dia_imp_box_of_atMostOne, nothing_contingent.

This is the precise modal content of determinism, and it also shows why the existence/uniqueness distinction of §4 is not pedantry: the two conjuncts of |ℋ| = 1 do different modal work, and only both together collapse the modalities.

Note also what the collapse is relative to. It is indexed to L, to t, and to s. Nothing collapses if any index is allowed to vary — which is exactly the content of §17.

17Alternative Histories

"Could the universe have evolved differently?" has three readings, and determinism forecloses only one. This is the most practically important result in the report, because ordinary counterfactual reasoning runs almost entirely on the readings that survive.

Theorem 7 · Which counterfactuals survive determinism MACHINE

A — same prior state, same laws. Foreclosed. This is exactly what |ℋ| = 1 denies.

B — different prior state, same laws. Survives. A fully deterministic law can admit many histories through different states. Determinism says nothing about how many possible worlds there are; only that they do not intersect and diverge.

C — same prior state, different laws. Survives. Two deterministic laws can share a state and diverge thereafter.

readingB_survives_determinism, readingC_survives_determinism, twoWorlds_deterministic.

The countermodel for B is worth stating, since it is counterintuitive: take a law whose possible histories are the constant-true and constant-false histories. It is deterministic under Definition 4 — the two histories never agree at any time, so the antecedent of the definition is never satisfied — while there are plainly two possible histories, reachable from two different initial states. Determinism restricts how histories may relate to one another; it does not restrict how many there are. MACHINE

Now consider what ordinary "could have done otherwise" talk requires. "I could have caught the train" is not usually a claim that the exact microphysical state of the universe permitted two continuations. It is a claim about what would have happened had I left earlier — reading B, a different prior state. Almost all deliberation, planning, regret, and counterfactual explanation is reading B. Determinism leaves every bit of it intact. It is only the philosopher's reading A — identical past, identical laws, different outcome — that |ℋ| = 1 touches. DERIVED

18Consequences for Libertarian Free Will

Treating agents abstractly as physical systems embedded in the universe, with no appeal to neuroscience: the question is what follows logically from the premise.

18.1 The core argument, evaluated step by step

Premise 1. The universe has a complete physical state St. — Not established, and possibly not well-formed at the fundamental level (§8). This is the weakest step in the argument and it is usually granted without comment.

Premise 2. The laws L determine the allowed evolution of St. — Ambiguous between P1 and P2. On the P1 reading it is uncontroversial and too weak; on the P2 reading it is exactly what §§5–14 found to be theory-dependent.

Premise 3. Given St and L, exactly one physically possible history exists. — Conditional (Verdict C). Not established for the actual universe.

Conclusion 1. No physically possible alternative history with the same prior state and laws. — Follows trivially from Premise 3 (Theorem 3). Zero axioms.

Conclusion 2. Any event in that history has no physically possible alternative outcome under identical prior conditions. — Follows only given an extra premise, which the argument as stated does not make explicit.

Theorem 4 · The hidden premise MACHINE

If an action-ascription is a function of the physical history, uniqueness of the history entails uniqueness of the action. But if the action depends on any further parameter not fixed by the physical history, a unique physical history is compatible with distinct actions.

So Conclusion 2 requires the supervenience of action on physical history — equivalently, causal closure of the physical. That premise is substantive, is precisely what interactionist dualism and strong emergentism deny, and is doing real work in the argument.

no_alternative_action, alternative_action_without_supervenience.

This is not an endorsement of dualism — supervenience on the physical is well supported independently. It is an accounting correction: the argument has three premises, not two, and the third should be stated.

Conclusion 3. If libertarian free will requires the genuine possibility of an alternative outcome under identical prior conditions, then it is incompatible with the deterministic premise. — Valid, and analytic.

18.2 Why Conclusion 3 is less interesting than it looks

Conclusion 3 is a one-line consequence of Theorem 3 plus the stated definition of libertarian free will. The physics contributes nothing to the inference; it contributes only to whether the antecedent holds. If you define libertarian free will as requiring reading-A alternatives, then determinism excludes it by construction — this is a fact about the definition, not a discovery about the world.

The genuinely informative results are elsewhere:

Indeterminism does not help. Suppose |ℋ| > 1 — a GRW world. Rewind to St and let it run again; sometimes the agent does A, sometimes B. The difference is not settled by anything about the agent — that is what it means for the transition to be chancy. So the agent has no more control than in the deterministic case; the outcome is simply less predictable. This is the rollback or luck objection, associated with van Inwagen and with Mele ("Ultimate Responsibility and Dumb Luck", in Responsibility, Cambridge, 1999, 274–293), and it is the reason that "quantum indeterminacy makes room for free will" fails. BIBLIO The point generalises: leeway-libertarian free will is threatened by determinism and by indeterminism alike, and the threat is structural rather than physical. DERIVED

The Consequence Argument, and its cost. Van Inwagen's argument (developed from "The incompatibility of free will and determinism", Philosophical Studies 27(3), 1975, 185–199, into An Essay on Free Will, 1983) runs: if determinism is true, our acts are consequences of the laws and the remote past; neither is up to us; so the consequences are not up to us. BIBLIO Lewis's "Are We Free to Break the Laws?" (Theoria 47(3), 1981, 113–121) replies by distinguishing the ability to falsify a law from the ability to do something such that a law would have been different — denying the first and claiming only the second. (The earlier draft of this report said "conceding the first", which inverts him: the ability actually to violate a law is the one Lewis refuses.) The Stanford Encyclopedia's Compatibilism entry, §4.1.2, draws the line the same way — between "a person who has the ability to act in such a way that she violates a law of nature, as opposed to a person (at a deterministic world) who has the ability to act in such a way that, if she were to so act, some law of nature that does obtain would not," noting that "the former ability would require magical powers" while the latter "would require nothing outlandish", and naming Lewis 1981 as the notable proponent of the second. SOURCED Notice that this dispute is entirely about the modal semantics of "up to us"; no physical result bears on it.

Alternative possibilities may not be required anyway. Frankfurt's 1969 cases ("Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility", Journal of Philosophy 66(23), 829–839) aim to show an agent can be morally responsible while unable to do otherwise — a counterfactual intervener who would have forced the act had the agent not performed it, but who never intervenes. His target, in the Stanford Encyclopedia's statement of it, is the "Principle of Alternate Possibilities (a person is morally responsible for what he has done only if he could have done otherwise)", and his aim was "to defend the claim that moral responsibility is compatible with determinism without having to defend the claim that the ability to do otherwise is compatible with determinism." SOURCED If sound, the principle of alternate possibilities is false, and the entire determinism-versus-free-will debate has been fought over the wrong requirement. Source incompatibilists (Pereboom, "Determinism al Dente", Noûs 29(1), 1995, 21–45) accordingly relocate the incompatibility from leeway to sourcehood: what matters is whether the agent is the ultimate origin of the act, not whether alternatives were available. Determinism threatens that too, but by a different route, and one on which the physics is again not decisive. BIBLIO

What the physics actually settles about free will

Very little, and less than either side usually claims. It settles a conditional whose consequent was built into a definition. It does not settle the antecedent (Verdict B). And on the two questions that would matter — whether alternative possibilities are required for responsibility, and whether sourcehood can survive either determinism or chance — physics is silent, because these are questions about the analysis of agency, not about the size of ℋ.

A particular definition of physical possibility. Yes — nomological possibility indexed to a state (§15). Under other readings of "possible", the answer changes. Verdict: stated explicitly, so not hidden, but it does mean the thesis is definition-relative.

21.2 The named fallacies

  • Determinism / fatalism. Fatalism says the outcome is fixed regardless of what you do; determinism says it is fixed partly through what you do. Under determinism your deliberation is a cause; under fatalism it is idle. Conflating them is the most common error in popular treatments.
  • Determinism / eternalism. Addressed in §7.4. The decisive case is a GRW block universe: fully real four-dimensional history, stochastic transitions.
  • Randomness / freedom. Addressed in §18.2. Chance supplies alternatives without supplying control.
  • Unpredictability / indeterminism. Addressed in §6. Chaos is the standing counterexample in one direction, the Norton dome in the other.
  • Equivocation on "history". Everett makes this concrete: one universal history, many branch histories, and a debate that looks substantive is partly a scope ambiguity (Theorem 5).
  • Circularity in the free-will step. Defining libertarian free will as requiring reading-A alternatives and then concluding that determinism excludes it is valid but empty — it is a definition being unpacked, not a discovery (§18.2).

21.3 The strongest objection to this report's own position

Stated at full force: the report retreats into conditionals. Every verdict is hedged on an unresolved interpretive question, and hedging is not a result. Physics has in fact been overwhelmingly successful with deterministic differential equations; the pathologies are measure-zero curiosities; the interpretive disputes have made no experimental difference in ninety years. The honest reading of the evidence is that the world is deterministic and the report is manufacturing doubt.

The reply has two parts, and the first partly concedes.

Conceded: the pathologies of §5 are non-generic, and if one restricts to Lipschitz forces with complete flows — which every working physicist does — classical mechanics is deterministic. The dome does not show that the actual world is indeterministic; it shows the formalism does not by itself force determinism.

Not conceded: the interpretive dispute is not idle. Bohmian mechanics, Everett and GRW are not readings of one theory; they are different theories with different ontologies, and GRW makes different experimental predictions in principle. That no experiment has yet discriminated them is a fact about current experimental reach, not evidence that the question is empty. And the deeper objection stands independently of quantum mechanics: §8's problem comes from general covariance, and it would still be there in a purely classical general-relativistic universe. The demand that Verdict B be upgraded from "unsupported" to "true" amounts to asking that a claim about the fundamental laws be settled before the fundamental laws are known.

22Synthesis

22.1 The three levels, kept separate

The brief requires that mathematical, physical and philosophical claims never be used as evidence for one another. The report's structure enforces this:

  • Level I — Mathematical. If a theory has unique solutions through each state, its histories are unique; local Lipschitz continuity suffices for local uniqueness; uniqueness does not give existence; determinism is description-relative and does not require an evolution operator. All established, most machine-checked.
  • Level II — Physical. Do our best theories have that property? Theory-dependent, and unresolved for the actual world. Nothing at Level I bears on this: that |ℋ| = 1 implies no alternatives is no evidence whatever that |ℋ| = 1.
  • Level III — Philosophical. What follows about possibility and freedom if it does hold? Conditional results only. And Level III must not be run backwards: that determinism would be uncomfortable for moral responsibility is not evidence that physics is indeterministic.

22.2 The four hypotheses

H1 — Strong determinism. The laws plus a complete state fix exactly one history. Not supported as a claim about the actual universe. It requires a complete state to be well-defined (§8), a determinate interpretation of quantum mechanics (§§11–14), and global existence (§4). None is secured.

H2 — Conditional determinism. Some major theories are deterministic; modern physics does not establish universal determinism. Best supported. It is exactly what the survey of §§5–14 yields, and it requires no interpretive bet.

H3 — Fundamental indeterminism. At least one fundamental aspect is genuinely stochastic. Live but unestablished. True if GRW-type collapse is correct; that is an open experimental question, and the only one on this list with a foreseeable experimental handle.

H4 — Determinism with branching. Deterministic universal dynamics with multiple emergent branches. Live, and the strongest contender if Everett is correct. Note it is not a rival to H1 so much as a demonstration that H1's key term is ambiguous: an Everettian world satisfies |ℋ| = 1 universally and |ℋ| > 1 branch-relatively.

H2 wins on the evidence. But the more useful observation is that H1, H3 and H4 are not currently discriminated by any experiment performed to date — they correspond to Bohm, GRW and Everett respectively, and the first and third are empirically equivalent to standard quantum mechanics by construction. The determinism question is, at present, underdetermined by evidence rather than answered by it.

22.3 The ten questions, answered independently

  1. What mathematically constitutes deterministic evolution? Uniqueness of the possible history through each state (Definition 4, Theorem 1) — not the existence of an evolution operator, which is sufficient but not necessary.
  2. Does deterministic evolution imply a unique physical history? No. It implies at most one. Existence is a separate conjunct and it fails in real models (Theorem 2).
  3. Do the major theories possess unique evolution? Conditionally: Newtonian and Hamiltonian mechanics on restricted model classes; general relativity up to isometry inside the maximal globally hyperbolic development; quantum mechanics only once the measurement problem is resolved, and then it depends how.
  4. Does quantum mechanics fundamentally undermine uniqueness? No. Unitary evolution is deterministic; only the collapse postulate is stochastic, and it is precisely the part no one regards as fundamental.
  5. Do Bohm, Everett, collapse or superdeterminism change the answer? Decisively. Bohm: deterministic, non-local. Everett: deterministic, level-relative. GRW: genuinely indeterministic. Superdeterminism: deterministic by construction, no worked-out model.
  6. Is there a rigorous concept of a complete state of the universe? In classical mechanics yes; in general relativity only for globally hyperbolic spacetimes and only as a constrained diffeomorphism class; in canonical quantum gravity plausibly not at all.
  7. Can there be two physically possible histories from a complete state? Yes, in some theories (GRW; Norton's dome; past a Cauchy horizon; space invaders). No, in others (Bohm; Everett universally). Not settled for the actual world.
  8. If there cannot, what follows about alternative possibilities? Reading A is foreclosed; readings B and C survive untouched (Theorem 7). Almost all ordinary counterfactual talk is reading B.
  9. Does this contradict libertarian free will? Leeway libertarianism, yes — analytically, given supervenience (Theorem 4). Source libertarianism is threatened differently. Compatibilism is untouched. And indeterminism rescues none of them.
  10. Which assumptions are established physics, mathematical theorems, and philosophical premises? Theorems: Theorems 1–8, Picard–Lindelöf, Choquet-Bruhat–Geroch, Bell, Xia. Established physics: Bell-inequality violation; the empirical adequacy of unitary quantum mechanics. Philosophical premises: that a complete instantaneous state exists; causal closure; the individuation of states; the definition of physical possibility; the analysis of "could have done otherwise".

23Final Verdict

To the question as posed — does the mathematical structure of physical law imply that a complete state and the laws admit exactly one possible continuation? — the answer is no, not as a matter of mathematical structure, and not established as a matter of physical fact.

Uniqueness is a contingent property that some mathematical models have and others lack, and which of them describes the world is not currently known. The proposition |ℋ(St, L)| = 1 is true of Bohmian mechanics and of Everettian quantum mechanics at the universal level, false of objective-collapse theories, conditional in classical mechanics and general relativity, and possibly ill-formed at the level of a quantum theory of gravity. Nothing in the mathematics forces it; nothing in the current evidence settles it.

Three results are worth carrying away independently of that verdict.

The inference everyone argues about is empty. From |ℋ| = 1 to "no alternatives" is a definitional unfolding requiring no axioms. Every dispute worth having is about the antecedent, and framing the debate around the inference is what keeps it unproductive.

The question presupposes more than physics supplies. It requires a complete instantaneous state (not available in general relativity, plausibly absent in quantum gravity), a fixed individuation of states (not settled by the formalism — the hole argument), and a fixed level of description (Everett shows the answer flips between levels). These are not quibbles; they are the reason the question resists a clean answer.

On "I genuinely could have done otherwise": if the deterministic premise holds, then under the identical-past-and-laws reading, no. That conclusion is analytic rather than empirical, it needs a supervenience premise that is usually left unstated, its antecedent is not established, and the alternative — genuine chance — supplies no control and so rescues nothing. The physics does not deliver the verdict that both sides of the free-will debate keep asking it for, and the reason is that the disagreement was never really about the size of ℋ.

theorem alternative_action_without_supervenience :

-- Determinism does not need an evolution operator: exactly one possible
-- history, yet no step function generates it.
def period3 : History Nat Bool := fun n => if n % 3 = 2 then false else true
def globalLaw : Law Nat Bool := ⟨fun h => h = period3⟩

theorem globalLaw_has_no_evolution :
    ¬ ∃ F : Bool → Bool, StepGoverned F globalLaw := by
  intro hex
  cases hex with
  | intro F hF =>
    have e0 : period3 1 = F (period3 0) := hF period3 rfl 0
    have e1 : period3 2 = F (period3 1) := hF period3 rfl 1
    have p0 : period3 0 = true := rfl
    have p1 : period3 1 = true := rfl
    have p2 : period3 2 = false := rfl
    rw [p0, p1] at e0
    rw [p1, p2] at e1
    rw [← e0] at e1
    exact Bool.noConfusion e1
