The Global–Maximal Gap

A scalar autonomous initial-value problem whose solution is unique among globally defined solutions and non-unique among maximal ones — because every non-trivial branch blows up in finite time.

Abstract

We exhibit and analyse a class of scalar autonomous initial-value problems ẋ = f(x), x(0) = 0, with f continuous, f(0) = 0 and f > 0 off the origin, in which three features occur together: uniqueness fails at the initial state, so that solutions may remain at 0 for an arbitrary waiting time and then depart; every departing solution is non-continuable with a bounded interval of existence, reaching +∞ in finite time; and the zero solution is defined for all time. Within that class the exact criterion for all three — necessary as well as sufficient — is a single convergence condition, T∗ = ∫0∞ du/f(u) < ∞, and T∗ is precisely the lifetime of a departing branch measured from its departure time.

The consequence is that the set of forward-global solutions through the origin is a singleton while the set of forward-maximal solutions — those admitting no extension within [0,∞) — has the cardinality of the continuum. The explicit witness f(x) = |x|2/3 + x² gives T∗ = 3√2 π/4 in closed form.

We keep the mathematical statement separate from its interpretation. The result concerns a scalar autonomous ordinary differential equation; no theorem about N-body Newtonian dynamics is proved here. What it does isolate is a distinction usually left implicit in discussions of determinism — between uniqueness among globally defined histories and uniqueness among maximal histories — and we argue that whether the configuration counts as a failure of determinism turns on a prior question about which solutions are admitted as complete physical histories. We also prove a small negative result: this mechanism cannot be realised on a surface of revolution in the manner of Norton's dome, because arc-length parametrisation bounds the tangential acceleration by g.

1Introduction

Discussions of determinism in classical physics routinely separate two ways an initial-value problem can fail to fix the future. Uniqueness can fail, so that one state admits several continuations; Norton's dome [9] is the standard example. Or existence can fail, so that a solution ceases to exist after a finite time; the non-collision singularities of Xia [11] and Xue [12] are the standard examples. Earman's survey treats the two in separate subsections [4, §3.5, §3.6], the second of which begins by assuming a Lipschitz condition and thereby assuming uniqueness away.

This note concerns systems in which the two occur together, and in a specific configuration: the branches that violate uniqueness are exactly the ones that cease to exist. The effect is that the two standard notions of "the possible histories through a state" come apart. Among solutions defined for all forward time there is exactly one. Among solutions that simply cannot be extended any further, there are continuum-many. Neither count is wrong; they answer different questions, and the difference is not visible unless the solution concept is fixed in advance.

Our aims are modest and we state them precisely. We prove a characterisation theorem for scalar autonomous equations (§4), give an explicit witness with a closed-form blow-up time (§6), extend to two-sided time under an additional hypothesis which we show is not removable (§7), place the construction against the examples in the literature we searched (§8), and discuss — without settling — whether the configuration should be described as indeterministic (§9). We prove no theorem about N-body dynamics, and we say what we have and have not established about mechanical realisation (§11).

2Definitions

Throughout, f : ℝ → ℝ and we study

ẋ = f(x),   x(0) = 0.(IVP)
Definition 1 · Solutions and intervals

A solution is a function x : I → ℝ, I ⊆ ℝ an interval with non-empty interior, which is differentiable on I and satisfies ẋ(t) = f(x(t)) for every t ∈ I (one-sided derivatives at endpoints of I belonging to I).

A solution x : I → ℝ is maximal, or non-continuable, if there is no solution y : J → ℝ with I ⊊ J ⊆ ℝ and y|I = x. Its I is its maximal interval. This is the standard two-sided notion, and maximal unqualified always carries it below.

A forward solution is a solution whose interval satisfies min I = 0, so I ⊆ [0,∞). It is forward-maximal if there is no forward solution y : J → ℝ with I ⊊ J ⊆ [0,∞) and y|I = x; its I is then its forward-maximal interval.

These are different properties, and the difference is not cosmetic. Forward-maximality quantifies only over extensions inside [0,∞); maximality quantifies over extensions in ℝ. A forward-maximal solution need not be maximal, because it may extend backward past 0, and for the witness of §6 none of them is: Proposition 9 shows that each extends backward in continuum-many ways. We therefore never drop the prefix, and we never call [0, T + T∗) a maximal interval.

Warning. When uniqueness fails there is no such thing as the maximal solution through a point, nor the forward-maximal one. We always write "a maximal solution".

Definition 2 · Global, and the two conventions

The word global is used inconsistently in the literature. We fix two senses and never mix them.

x is forward-global if it is a solution on [0, ∞).
x is two-sided global if it is a solution on all of ℝ.

The main theorem (§4) is stated for forward-global solutions. The two-sided version is Proposition 9 in §7 and requires a strictly stronger hypothesis.

Definition 3 · Finite-time blow-up

A solution x : I → ℝ with τ = sup I < ∞ blows up at τ if |x(t)| → ∞ as t → τ−. A solution that blows up at τ admits no extension past τ in either sense, so the notion is neutral between the two maximality conventions of Definition 1.

We avoid the word incomplete for solutions; in dynamical systems it is standard for a vector field (one all of whose maximal solutions are defined on ℝ is complete). We also avoid non-unique continuation, which collides with the unrelated unique continuation property of partial differential equations, and speak instead of failure of uniqueness for the initial-value problem.

Definition 4 · The two history sets

For (IVP), set

ℋglob(0) = { x : [0,∞) → ℝ  |  x solves (IVP) }
ℋmax+(0) = { x  |  x is a forward-maximal solution of (IVP) }

Both are sets of forward solutions, and ℋglob(0) ⊆ ℋmax+(0), since a solution on [0,∞) admits no forward extension inside [0,∞).

The superscript + is load-bearing. Writing ℋmax(0) for a set of solutions with domains in [0,∞) would be an error, not an abbreviation: under (H1) alone, no solution of (IVP) whose domain lies in [0,∞) is maximal in the sense of Definition 1, since setting x(t) = 0 for t < 0 always extends it — f(0) = 0, so the zero solution runs backward without bound. The maximal solutions in the standard sense are classified separately, in Proposition 9.

Standing hypotheses

(H1) f is continuous on ℝ, f(0) = 0, and f(x) > 0 for every x ≠ 0.
(H2) ∫01 du/f(u) < ∞.    (H3) ∫1∞ du/f(u) < ∞.
(H4) S∗ = ∫−∞0 du/f(u) < ∞  (needed only in §7), that is, both
    (H4a) ∫−10 du/f(u) < ∞    and    (H4b) ∫−∞−1 du/f(u) < ∞.

Under (H1) the integrands are positive and continuous on the relevant open intervals, so each integral is well defined in (0, ∞]. Write T∗ = ∫0∞ du/f(u); then T∗ < ∞ iff (H2) and (H3) both hold.

Neither half is new in isolation, and we state where each comes from. (H3) is the negation of the classical no-blow-up criterion: Hartman [6, ch. III, Thm 5.1, p. 30] shows that if |f| ≤ ψ(|y|) with ψ > 0 continuous and ∫∞ du/ψ(u) = ∞, then solutions exist on the whole strip — so blow-up requires convergence. (H2) is the negation of the classical uniqueness criterion in the Osgood [10] and Kamke line, as given in Hartman [6, ch. III §6]. What this note does is put the two negations together and read off the consequence, which is §4.

(H4) is the exact mirror of (H2)-and-(H3) across the origin: (H4a) is what lets a branch reach the origin from below, (H4b) is what makes its backward lifetime finite. (H2) says nothing about the negative half-line, so (H4a) is a genuine additional assumption and not a consequence of (H2): take f(u) = u2/3 for u > 0 and f(u) = |u| for u < 0, which is continuous and satisfies (H1) and (H2), while ∫−10 du/f(u) = ∫01 dv/v = ∞. For an even f — such as the witness of §6 — (H4a) follows from (H2) and (H4b) from (H3), but Proposition 9 is not stated only for even f.

3The mechanism

Two lemmas carry everything. The first says that under (H1) the solution structure is rigid: every solution is monotone, so a solution through the origin can only rest at 0 on an interval and then leave. The second identifies the departing branch and its lifetime.

Lemma 5 · Monotonicity and the zero set

Assume (H1). Then every solution x : I → ℝ is non-decreasing, and Z = { t ∈ I : x(t) = 0 } is a closed subinterval of I (possibly empty).

Proof By (H1), f ≥ 0 on ℝ, so ẋ(t) = f(x(t)) ≥ 0 for all t ∈ I and x is non-decreasing. Z = x−1({0}) is closed in I because x is continuous. If t1, t2 ∈ Z and t1 < t < t2, monotonicity gives 0 = x(t1) ≤ x(t) ≤ x(t2) = 0, so t ∈ Z; hence Z is an interval. ∎
Lemma 6 · The departing branch

Assume (H1) and (H2). Define F(x) = ∫0x du/f(u) for x > 0. Then:

(i) F is a C1 strictly increasing bijection from (0, ∞) onto (0, T∗), with F(0+) = 0.

(ii) Its inverse φ = F−1, extended by φ(0) = 0, is C1 on [0, T∗), satisfies φ′ = f(φ) there, and φ′(0) = f(0) = 0.

(iii) φ(t) → +∞ as t → T∗−; hence if T∗ < ∞ the forward-maximal interval of φ is [0, T∗) and φ blows up in finite time.

(iv) Uniqueness of the branch. If ψ is any solution with ψ(t0) = 0 and ψ > 0 on (t0, t1], then ψ(t) = φ(t − t0) on [t0, t1].

Proof (i) On (0,∞) the integrand 1/f is positive and continuous, so F is C1 with F′ = 1/f > 0, hence strictly increasing. (H2) gives F(0+) = 0, and by definition F(x) → T∗ as x → ∞. Continuity and strict monotonicity give the bijection onto (0, T∗).

(ii) On (0, T∗) the inverse function theorem gives φ′(t) = 1/F′(φ(t)) = f(φ(t)). At t = 0: since φ(t) → 0 as t → 0+ and f is continuous with f(0) = 0, we get φ′(t) = f(φ(t)) → 0. By the mean value theorem, for t > 0 there is ξt ∈ (0,t) with (φ(t) − φ(0))/t = φ′(ξt), and ξt → 0, so the right derivative of φ at 0 exists and equals 0 = f(φ(0)). Thus φ′ is continuous on [0, T∗).

(iii) Immediate from (i): φ = F−1 and F(x) → T∗ only as x → ∞. No continuation past T∗ is possible since φ is unbounded near T∗.

(iv) On (t0, t1] we have f(ψ) > 0, so ψ is strictly increasing there and ψ̇/f(ψ) = 1. Integrating from s to t1 and substituting u = ψ(σ) gives ∫ψ(s)ψ(t1) du/f(u) = t1 − s. Letting s ↓ t0 and using continuity of ψ and (H2), F(ψ(t1)) = t1 − t0. The same argument at every t ∈ (t0, t1] gives F(ψ(t)) = t − t0, i.e. ψ(t) = φ(t − t0). ∎

Why no Lipschitz hypothesis appears

Lemma 6(iv) gives uniqueness of the branch after it has left the origin, and the proof uses only that f > 0 there, which makes the solution strictly monotone and hence invertible. This is special to scalar autonomous equations. Failure of uniqueness for (IVP) is therefore located entirely at one point — the choice of departure time — and not in any ambiguity about the branch itself.

4Main theorem

Theorem 7 · Forward time

Assume (H1), (H2), (H3), and let T∗ = ∫0∞ du/f(u) < ∞. For T ∈ [0, ∞) define

xT(t) = 0 for 0 ≤ t ≤ T,   xT(t) = φ(t − T) for T < t < T + T∗,

and let x∞ ≡ 0 on [0,∞). Then:

(a) Each xT, T < ∞, is a solution of (IVP) with forward-maximal interval [0, T + T∗), and xT(t) → +∞ as t → (T + T∗)−.

(b) x∞ is a solution on [0,∞).

(c) ℋmax+(0) = { xT : T ∈ [0, ∞] }, and T ↦ xT is injective.

(d) Consequently

| ℋglob(0) | = 1   and   | ℋmax+(0) | = 𝔠,

where 𝔠 is the cardinality of the continuum.

Proof (a) On [0,T) and on (T, T+T∗), xT solves the equation by construction and Lemma 6(ii). At t = T the left derivative is 0 and the right derivative is φ′(0) = 0 by Lemma 6(ii); since f(xT(T)) = f(0) = 0, the equation holds at T and xT ∈ C1. Blow-up and forward-maximality are Lemma 6(iii).

(b) f(0) = 0.

(c) Let x be a forward-maximal solution of (IVP), with interval I ⊆ [0,∞), 0 ∈ I. By Lemma 5 its zero set Z is a closed subinterval of I containing 0, so Z = I ∩ [0,T] with T = sup Z ∈ [0,∞]. Three cases.

  (i) T = ∞. Then x ≡ 0 on I, and forward-maximality forces I = [0,∞), so x = x∞.

  (ii) T < ∞ and T ∉ I. Then sup I = T and Z = I, so x ≡ 0 on I = [0,T); but extending x by 0 to [0,∞) is a forward solution properly extending it, contradicting forward-maximality. This case does not occur.

  (iii) T < ∞ and T ∈ I. Then x(T) = 0 and, by Lemma 5 again, x > 0 on I ∩ (T, ∞) — it cannot be negative, since x is non-decreasing and vanishes at T. Lemma 6(iv) gives x(t) = φ(t − T) there, so I ⊆ [0, T + T∗) by Lemma 6(iii), and forward-maximality gives equality. So x = xT.

Injectivity: T∗ > 0 because 1/f is positive, so for T < T′ the interval (T, min(T′, T + T∗)) has positive length, and on it xT > 0 = xT′.

(d) By (a), every xT with T < ∞ has bounded domain, so is not forward-global; by (b) and (c), ℋglob(0) = { x∞ }. By (c) the map [0,∞] → ℋmax+(0) is a bijection, and |[0,∞]| = 𝔠. ∎

Sharpness. Both hypotheses are doing work, and — this is the half that makes the criterion exact rather than merely sufficient — each failure is fatal on its own.

Necessity

Assume (H1) only.

(i) If (H2) fails then x ≡ 0 is the only forward solution of (IVP), so |ℋmax+(0)| = |ℋglob(0)| = 1.

(ii) If (H2) holds and (H3) fails then T∗ = ∞, every xT is forward-global, and |ℋglob(0)| = |ℋmax+(0)| = 𝔠.

Proof (i) Suppose some forward solution x has x(t1) > 0. The set { t ∈ [0,t1] : x(t) = 0 } is non-empty (it contains 0) and closed, so T = sup of it is attained and T < t1. Then x > 0 on (T, t1], so the computation of Lemma 6(iv) applies on [s, t1] for each s ∈ (T, t1) and gives ∫x(s)x(t1) du/f(u) = t1 − s ≤ t1 − T. As s ↓ T we have x(s) → 0 by continuity, and the integrals increase, so by monotone convergence ∫0x(t1) du/f(u) ≤ t1 − T < ∞. Since 1/f is continuous and positive on every compact subinterval of (0,∞), this forces ∫01 du/f(u) < ∞, i.e. (H2) — a contradiction. Hence no forward solution ever leaves the origin, and by Lemma 5 none goes negative either.

(ii) If ∫1∞ du/f = ∞ then F(x) → ∞ as x → ∞, so φ = F−1 is defined on all of [0,∞) and each xT is a solution on [0,∞). The counting is Theorem 7(c), whose proof used (H3) only to identify the right endpoint. ∎

Note that (i) uses no hypothesis beyond (H1): it is the Osgood-type necessity argument, and it is why "T∗ < ∞" is the exact criterion within the class (H1) and not merely a sufficient condition. Nothing here says that T∗ < ∞ is sufficient on its own: continuity, f(0) = 0 and positivity off the origin are all used, and dropping any of them changes the conclusion.

5Power-law corollary — and a warning

The hypotheses (H2) and (H3) are convergence conditions on 1/f at the two ends. They are not equivalent to any statement about the growth ratio f(u)/u, and we record this explicitly because the ratio formulation is tempting and false.

Proposition 8 · The ratio conditions are strictly weaker

(i) (H2) implies limsupu→0+ f(u)/u = +∞, and the converse fails.

(ii) (H3) implies limsupu→∞ f(u)/u = +∞, and the converse fails.

Proof (i) If f(u) ≤ Cu on (0, δ) then ∫0δ du/f(u) ≥ C−1∫0δ du/u = ∞, contradicting (H2). For the converse, take f(u) = u/log(1/u) on (0, 1/2). Then f(u)/u = 1/log(1/u) → 0, yet with s = log(1/u),

∫01/2 du/f(u) = ∫01/2 log(1/u)·u−1 du = ∫log 2∞ s ds = ∞.

(ii) If f(u) ≤ Cu for large u then ∫∞ du/f = ∞. For the converse, take f(u) = u log u on (2, ∞): f(u)/u → ∞, yet ∫2∞ du/(u log u) = [log log u]2∞ = ∞. ∎

Attribution. The example in (ii) is not ours. Hartman [6, ch. III §5, Remark 1, p. 30] lists ψ(u) = Cu and Cu log u as admissible majorants in a criterion that forbids blow-up, which is exactly the observation that u log u grows superlinearly while ∫∞ du/(u log u) still diverges. We reproduce it because the point it makes is easy to lose.

What is available is a corollary under power-law asymptotics. Write g ≍ h for 0 < liminf g/h ≤ limsup g/h < ∞.

Corollary (power laws only)

If f(u) ≍ up as u → 0+ with p > 0, then (H2) holds iff p < 1.
If f(u) ≍ uq as u → ∞ with q > 0, then (H3) holds iff q > 1.

A terminological caution: the exponent p = 2/3 is smaller than 1, but u2/3 > u for 0 < u < 1. Calling this "sublinear" is standard for the exponent and misleading for the function. We therefore state hypotheses as integrals throughout and use exponents only inside this corollary.

7Two-sided extension

The forward theorem says nothing about x < 0, and for the witness the negative half-line is genuinely populated: since f is even, (H2) gives (H4a) and solutions can also arrive at the origin from below in finite time. This section accounts for them, and in doing so settles what the maximal solutions of (IVP) actually are in the standard sense of Definition 1 — which, as the next proposition shows, is not what §4 classified.

Proposition 9 · Two-sided classification

Assume (H1), (H2), (H3), (H4), so that T∗ < ∞ and S∗ = ∫−∞0 du/f(u) < ∞. Then the map sending (S,T) ∈ [0,∞]² to the solution with zero set [−S, T] is a bijection onto the set of maximal solutions of (IVP) — maximal in the standard sense of Definition 1 — and the solution indexed by (S,T) has maximal interval

(−S − S∗,   T + T∗),

with the convention that S = ∞ replaces the left endpoint by −∞ and T = ∞ replaces the right endpoint by +∞. Consequently the unique two-sided global solution is x ≡ 0 (the case S = T = ∞), and the set of maximal solutions has cardinality 𝔠.

Proof sketch Lemma 5 gives that the zero set is a closed interval [−S,T] containing 0, so S, T ∈ [0,∞]. Forward of T, Lemma 6 and the case analysis of Theorem 7(c) apply verbatim and give the right endpoint T + T∗.

Backward of −S, set y(t) = −x(−t). Then ẏ(t) = ẋ(−t) = f(x(−t)) = f̃(y(t)) with f̃(y) = f(−y), and f̃ again satisfies (H1). Substituting u = −v, ∫01 dv/f̃(v) = ∫−10 du/f(u) and ∫1∞ dv/f̃(v) = ∫−∞−1 du/f(u): so (H4a) is (H2) for f̃, supplying the arriving branch, and (H4b) is (H3) for f̃, making its backward lifetime S∗ finite. Theorem 7 applied to f̃ then gives the left endpoint −S − S∗.

The two branches are glued at the zero interval exactly as in Theorem 7(a), the join being C1 at both ends by Lemma 6(ii). Injectivity: if T < T′ the two solutions differ on (T, min(T′, T + T∗)), and symmetrically for S. A solution is defined on all of ℝ iff both endpoints are infinite, i.e. iff S = T = ∞, i.e. iff x ≡ 0. Finally |[0,∞]²| = 𝔠. ∎

(H4) is not removable

Let f(x) = |x|2/3 for x ≤ 0 and f(x) = x2/3 + x² for x > 0. This f is continuous and satisfies (H1), (H2), (H3) and also (H4a) — indeed ∫−10 dx/|x|2/3 = 3 — but ∫−∞−1 dx/|x|2/3 = ∞, so (H4b) fails and with it (H4). Then S∗ = ∞, and the solution with (S,T) = (0,∞) — negative for t < 0, identically zero for t ≥ 0 — is defined on all of ℝ. Hence there are at least two two-sided global solutions and Proposition 9 fails.

Forward-maximal is not maximal

Combining Theorem 7(c) with Proposition 9: for every S ∈ [0,∞], the restriction to [0,∞) of the maximal solution indexed by (S,T) is exactly xT, since the zero set meets [0,∞) in [0,T] regardless of S. So each of the continuum-many forward-maximal solutions of §4 extends backward in continuum-many ways, and none of them is maximal in the sense of Definition 1. In particular [0, T + T∗) is a forward-maximal interval and not a maximal interval, and ℋmax+(0) is not a set of maximal solutions. This is the reason for the notation, and a referee is right to insist on it.

The gap of the title survives the distinction: it is a gap between ℋglob(0) and ℋmax+(0) in forward time, and between the two-sided global solutions (one) and the maximal solutions (continuum-many) in Proposition 9. Both comparisons hold; they are different comparisons and we do not conflate them.

On lifetimes. T∗ is the lifetime of a forward branch measured from its departure time, not the lifetime of a two-sided solution. A maximal solution with finite S and T exists for a total time S∗ + (S + T) + T∗, comprising a backward branch, a waiting interval, and a forward branch. Departure time, arrival time, waiting length and blow-up time are four distinct quantities and we keep them distinct.

8Relation to known examples

We compare against the examples we examined, using four conditions:

(C1) several solutions of the same initial-value problem through one state;
(C2) every non-trivial branch terminates at a finite time — it is non-continuable and its interval of existence is bounded;
(C3) at least one forward-global solution through that same state;
(C4) the non-uniqueness is genuine for solutions of the same problem, not produced by enlarging the solution concept.

Shock waves, in [4] §15C1✓ C2✓ C3✗ C4✗The nearest miss. Ordinary solutions fail to exist after a finite time; uniqueness is then lost because weak solutions are admitted. The two failures are sequential, and the non-uniqueness arises from changing the solution concept. C4 fails.
Berndl et al. [2]C1✗ C2✓ — C4✓Catalogues, for Bohmian trajectories, both escape to infinity in finite time and singular points of the velocity field. But at a node the field is unbounded, so "the solution would break down" — non-existence, not branching. C1 fails. (They also note that failure of essential self-adjointness makes the evolution of the wave function non-unique; that is a different phenomenon, concerning which operator the theory is about.)

Earman raises a closely related question at [4] §7: discussing non-collision singularities, he writes that it is not clear to what extent such solutions undermine determinism, "for it is not obvious under what conditions such solutions can be joined onto a normal solution." That question concerns the Newtonian N-body problem, and nothing here answers it. Our construction is a scalar autonomous equation in which the joining is exhibited explicitly, which is a much weaker statement.

Novelty, stated as what it is

The construction of this note satisfies all four: C1 and C3 by Theorem 7(c)–(d), C2 by Theorem 7(a) with Lemma 6(iii), and C4 because every xT is a solution in the single sense of Definition 1 — no weak, distributional or otherwise generalised solutions are admitted anywhere in this note.

In the literature searched for this project, we found no example satisfying C1–C4 simultaneously. The search covered Earman [4] ch. III in full text, Norton [9], Malament [7], Fletcher [5], Korolev (abstracts), Xia [11], Xue [12], Mather–McGehee [8] as reported in [4], Berndl et al. [2], Alexander [1], and keyword searches of the differential-equations literature on Osgood-type uniqueness criteria and on finite-time blow-up. This is a bounded search finding, not a proof of priority. The relevant conditions are classical — Osgood's uniqueness criterion [10] dates from 1898 — and Hartman [6] was available only as a page scan; we read ch. III §§5–6 (pp. 30–31) directly and found both halves of our criterion there in their classical form — Theorem 5.1 for blow-up and §6 for Kamke-type uniqueness — but not the combination, and we did not read the rest of the chapter or the rest of the book. Coddington and Levinson [3] was searched by keyword only, not read. A precedent, particularly as an exercise, would not surprise us.

9Is this indeterminism?

We do not think this question has an obvious answer, and we set out the competing readings rather than choosing.

The situation is: a state x = 0; exactly one solution through it that is defined for all forward time; continuum-many forward-maximal solutions through it, all but one of which cease to exist at a finite time. Five distinct things are in play and are easy to run together.

  1. Mathematical non-uniqueness of the initial-value problem. Unambiguous: it holds, by Theorem 7(c).
  2. Uniqueness among globally defined histories. Also unambiguous: it holds, by Theorem 7(d).
  3. Existence of maximal alternatives. Holds, and they are not pathological objects — each is a perfectly good C1 solution on its interval.
  4. Physical determinism. A thesis about physically possible histories, which requires (5) to be settled first.
  5. Whether an incomplete history is a complete physical history. The crux.

The reading on which determinism survives

If a physical history must be defined at every time, then the branches are not histories at all, and the state x = 0 has exactly one future. The alternatives are artefacts of a permissive solution concept, in the same way that a solution defined only on [0,1) is not a candidate history of a world that persists. On this reading the example shows something about the initial-value problem and nothing about determinism, and the appropriate response is to define possible histories as the globally defined solutions and observe that they are unique.

The reading on which determinism fails

This reading objects that the totality requirement is doing the work and is not independently motivated. Requiring histories to be total makes finite-time blow-up into non-existence rather than indeterminism, which already looks like a stipulation; and it has the consequence that a law can be "deterministic" while admitting no possible history at all. Further, the branches are not merely formal: an observer stationed at the origin would have no way to rule them out at any finite time, since every branch agrees with the zero solution on an initial interval of arbitrary length. On this reading, restricting to global solutions is a way of legislating determinism rather than establishing it.

The reading on which the question is malformed

A third response holds that "is it deterministic?" is not well posed until the solution concept is fixed, and that the example simply demonstrates that the two natural choices give different answers for the same law. On this reading the interest is diagnostic: it shows that the phrase "the possible histories through a state" is ambiguous in a way that can change a determinism verdict, not merely an existence verdict. This is the reading we find most defensible, but we record it as a position and not as a result.

One observation that seems robust across all three. The distinction here cannot be dissolved by demanding more regularity of f, or more physics. The witness is continuous everywhere and C1 away from a single point; the failure of local Lipschitz continuity occurs only at the equilibrium; and the branches blow up because the equation is superlinear at infinity, which is ordinary behaviour. Whatever the right verdict, it is not reached by ruling out pathology.

10Limitations

  • Scope. Everything proved here concerns a scalar autonomous ordinary differential equation. Lemma 5 uses the scalar order structure and Lemma 6(iv) uses scalar monotonicity. Neither survives to systems without new arguments, and we prove nothing about N-body Newtonian dynamics.
  • Not a physical theorem. Theorem 7 is a statement about an equation, not about a physical system. §11 discusses what a mechanical realisation would require.
  • Novelty. See the boxed statement in §8: a bounded literature-search finding, not a priority claim.
  • The two-sided case is conditional. Proposition 9 requires (H4), whose second half (H4b) we show is not removable. (H4a) is independent of (H2) and is also assumed, not derived.
  • Two maximality notions, deliberately kept apart. Theorem 7 classifies forward-maximal solutions — maximal among those with domain in [0,∞) — and Proposition 9 classifies maximal solutions in the standard two-sided sense. Neither result is about the other set, and the remark after Proposition 9 shows the two genuinely differ: no forward-maximal solution here is maximal.
  • The mechanical no-go is narrow. Proposition 10 covers one model — frictionless, uniform gravity, surface of revolution, arc-length meridional coordinate, zero angular momentum, particle remaining on the surface. It is not a claim about mechanical systems in general.
  • The realisation is of the mechanism, not the witness. Proposition 11 exhibits a Newtonian system in the class of Theorem 7; its reduced right-hand side is not |x|2/3 + x².
  • Interpretation is open. §9 presents three readings and endorses none as established.

11Mechanical realisation

It is natural to ask whether the mechanism can be produced by a Newtonian particle. We record one negative result and one positive one.

11.1 Not on a dome

Norton's construction places a particle on a frictionless surface of revolution, parametrised by arc length r along a meridian from the apex, with h(r) the depth below the apex. The Lagrangian is L = ½mṙ² + mgh(r), giving r̈ = g h′(r). Norton takes h(r) = (2/3g) r3/2, whence r̈ = √r.

An obvious candidate for our mechanism is h(r) = (1/g)(⅔r3/2 + ⅓r³), which does give r̈ = √r + r². It cannot be realised.

Proposition 10 · No finite-time escape in the Norton-type model

Consider the following model, which is Norton's. A point particle moves without friction on a surface of revolution embedded in Euclidean 3-space; uniform gravity g acts along the axis of the surface; r is arc length along a meridian from the apex and h(r) the depth below it; the motion is purely meridional, i.e. the angular momentum about the axis vanishes; and the particle remains on the surface throughout the motion considered.

Then the equation of motion is r̈ = g h′(r) with |h′(r)| ≤ 1, so |r̈| ≤ g; hence |ṙ(t)| ≤ |ṙ(0)| + g|t| and r(t) ≤ r(0) + |ṙ(0)||t| + ½g t² on the interval of existence. In particular r is bounded on every bounded time interval and cannot reach +∞ in finite time.

Proof Let the meridian be given in the half-plane by (ρ(r), z(r)) parametrised by arc length, so ρ′² + z′² = 1; since ρ′² ≥ 0 this forces |z′(r)| ≤ 1, and with h = −z, |h′(r)| ≤ 1. With zero angular momentum the Lagrangian is L = ½mṙ² + mgh(r), whose Euler–Lagrange equation is r̈ = g h′(r); hence |r̈| ≤ g. Integrating once from 0 to t gives |ṙ(t)| ≤ |ṙ(0)| + g|t| and once more gives the stated bound on r(t), which is finite at every finite t. So r stays bounded on bounded time intervals and no finite-time escape occurs. ∎

For the candidate above, h′(r) = (√r + r²)/g exceeds 1 once √r + r² > g — with g = 9.8, for r ≳ 2.85 — so no such surface exists beyond that radius. The same bound applies to Norton's own dome, which is why it is presented as a dome of finite extent; for Norton nothing is lost, since only local behaviour near the apex is needed. For us the escape requires r → ∞, so the obstruction is fatal for this route.

What Proposition 10 does not say

The bound uses all of the stated hypotheses, and each can fail:

  • Zero angular momentum. With angular momentum pθ ≠ 0 the meridional equation acquires a centrifugal term,
    r̈ = g h′(r) + (pθ²/m²)·ρ′(r)/ρ(r)³,
    which is not bounded by g — it is unbounded as ρ → 0. We make no claim about that case.
  • The particle stays on the surface. A particle resting on a surface (as opposed to a bead threaded on a wire) leaves it when the required normal force changes sign; beyond that point the equation no longer governs the motion.
  • Arc-length parametrisation. |h′| ≤ 1 is a fact about the arc-length coordinate. In another radial coordinate the analogous derivative is unbounded and the argument says nothing.

So the proposition closes one specific route — adapting Norton's dome by steepening it — and no more. We do not claim that constraint surfaces in general, or mechanical systems in general, cannot produce finite-time escape; §11.2 exhibits a mechanical system that does.

11.2 In a potential

Dropping the constraint surface and taking a particle of unit mass on a line in a potential removes the obstruction, at the cost of a potential unbounded below — the setting of [4] §3.6.

Proposition 11 · A one-dimensional realisation

Let V(x) = −(⅔ x3/2 + ⅓ x³) for x ≥ 0, and consider ẍ = −V′(x) = √x + x² with x(0) = ẋ(0) = 0. Then the solutions departing from rest at the origin satisfy the first-order equation ẋ = G(x), G(x) = (4/3·x3/2 + 2/3·x³)1/2, whose even extension Ĝ(x) = G(|x|) satisfies (H1), (H2), (H3). Consequently the conclusions of Theorem 7 apply: x ≡ 0 is the unique forward-global solution, and there are continuum-many forward-maximal solutions, departing at arbitrary times and blowing up after a further

T∗mech = ∫0∞ dx/G(x) = (2/3)·√(3/2)·2−1/3·B(⅙, ⅓) ≈ 5.4521363617.

Proof Energy conservation from rest at the origin gives ½ẋ² = −V(x), i.e. ẋ = G(x) on any interval where ẋ > 0; conversely G G′ = d/dx(½G²) = √x + x², so solutions of ẋ = G(x) solve the second-order equation. G is continuous on [0,∞) with G(0) = 0 and G > 0 on (0,∞). To apply Theorem 7 verbatim — which is stated for an f defined on all of ℝ — use the even extension Ĝ(x) = G(|x|), which is continuous on ℝ and satisfies (H1); by Lemma 5 the forward solutions of ẋ = Ĝ(x) from the origin never take negative values, so they are exactly the forward solutions of ẋ = G(x), and V need only be defined on [0,∞). As x → 0+, G(x) ≍ x3/4 with exponent 3/4 < 1, giving (H2) by the Corollary of §5; as x → ∞, G(x) ≍ x3/2 with exponent 3/2 > 1, giving (H3). For the constant, write G = √(2/3)·x3/4(2 + x3/2)1/2, substitute w = x3/2 then w = 2v, and use ∫0∞ vp−1(1+v)−(p+q) dv = B(p,q) with p = ⅙, q = ⅓. ∎

What is realised, and what is not

Proposition 11 realises the mechanism characterised by Theorem 7 — failure of uniqueness at an equilibrium, finite-time blow-up of every departing branch, exactly one forward-global solution — in a Newtonian system. It does not realise the witness of §6. The reduced equation is ẋ = G(x) with G(x) = (4/3·x3/2 + 2/3·x³)1/2, which is not |x|2/3 + x²; correspondingly T∗mech ≈ 5.4521 differs from T∗ ≈ 3.3322. The witness and the realisation are two different members of the class the theorem describes, and we do not claim that |x|2/3 + x² itself arises from a mechanical system.

We record two caveats. First, this is a particle in a potential unbounded below, which is exactly the idealisation that attracts the standard objection to escape solutions — that it draws on an infinitely deep well. Second, V is C1 but not C2 at the origin, which is what permits the departure; the force √x + x² is continuous and not locally Lipschitz there. Whether such a force law should count as a legitimate Newtonian system is the same question that the dome literature asks about √r [5, 7, 9], and we take no position on it here.

12Conclusion

Within the class (H1) — scalar autonomous equations with a continuous right-hand side vanishing at an equilibrium and positive off it — the convergence of a single integral ∫0∞ du/f(u) is necessary and sufficient for a configuration in which uniqueness fails at the equilibrium, every non-trivial branch terminates in finite time, and exactly one solution is defined for all forward time. The witness |x|2/3 + x² realises it with blow-up time 3√2π/4.

The mathematical content is elementary and the proofs are short. What we think is worth attention is the distinction it isolates: uniqueness among globally defined histories and uniqueness among histories that merely cannot be extended further are different properties of the same law, and they can disagree. Discussions of determinism that speak of "the possible histories through a state" without saying which class is meant are, in the presence of this mechanism, ambiguous in a way that changes the verdict.
